# Gender and Warrior Composition in the Late-Sixteenth-Century Philippines

**Research date:** 2026-07-29

**Depicted period:** principally 1570–1600, with earlier contact-era and
early-seventeenth-century evidence used only when clearly identified

**Purpose:** determine whether Hukbo's ordinary battle roster should be
understood as male, female, or mixed

## Finding

The surviving accounts consistently describe **organized fighting as
normatively male**. They do **not** support the stronger claim that every
person who ever fought in every late-sixteenth-century Philippine conflict
was male, and they do not provide percentages from which to measure the sex
composition of a force.

Use the following evidence labels:

- **Documented:** chiefs led armed followings; men fulfilled organized war,
  raiding, rowing, and shipboard fighting obligations; warrior prestige in the
  Visayas was closely tied to manhood.
- **Documented:** women took part in the ritual and household response to
  warfare while male kin were away.
- **Documented, form uncertain:** women and children could be present in an
  attacked settlement, and a modern peer-reviewed reading of an early conquest
  account includes them in improvised community defence. The available account
  does not establish their number, equipment, status, or whether this was
  typical.
- **Provisional reconstruction:** a routine mixed-gender expeditionary force,
  a female warrior class, female-only formations, or any numerical female
  combatant percentage.

This is therefore a distinction between an **ordinary roster** and an
**exception under direct attack**, not a claim that women were absent from
warfare.

## Evidence

### 1. Organized war service is described as male

Miguel de Loarca's 1582 account of the Visayas makes the clearest explicit
distinction. In describing wartime observance, he says that married men go to
war while women suspend work during their absence. Elsewhere, his account
describes the chief leading a raid and timaguas accompanying him as armed
oarsmen. This is direct evidence for a gendered division in organized,
expeditionary warfare, though it is not a census of every combatant.

**Label:** **Documented**

**Source:** [Miguel de Loarca, *Relacion de las Yslas Filipinas* (1582), in
Blair and Robertson, vol. 5](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/16501/pg16501-images.html)

Juan de Plasencia's 1589 account says a dato was a captain in war and that
maharlicas were obligated to accompany him at their own expense. The account
also discusses both male and female members of the maharlika social grade in
its rules for marriage and mobility. That does not prove women personally
fulfilled the grade's war obligation: social membership and combat duty are
not interchangeable categories.

**Label:** male-led service is **Documented**; female service cannot be inferred

**Source:** [Juan de Plasencia, *Customs of the Tagalogs* (1589), in Blair and
Robertson, vol. 7](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13701/pg13701-images.html)

Antonio de Morga's 1609 retrospective account repeatedly counts "fighting
men" in fleets and distinguishes fighting men on a vessel's raised platform
from its rowers. Because Morga wrote after the target period and often
described composite regional forces, this evidence corroborates a male norm
but should not be used alone to reconstruct a particular 1580s polity.

**Label:** **Documented**, retrospective corroboration

**Source:** [Antonio de Morga, *History of the Philippine Islands* (1609)](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7001/pg7001-images.html)

### 2. Warrior identity and manhood were closely linked in the Visayas

William Henry Scott's synthesis of sixteenth-century sources connects
war-earned tattooing, qualification as a warrior, and tests of manhood. He
also notes that some wartime refuges were intended specifically for male
warriors. These details describe a gendered warrior identity, not merely a
translation habit in one Spanish account.

Scott is strongest for the Visayas. His synthesis must not be converted into
a universal rule for every Philippine society.

**Label:** **Documented**, regionally bounded

**Source:** [William Henry Scott, *Barangay: Sixteenth-Century Philippine
Culture and Society* (1994)](https://archive.org/details/BarangaySixteenthCenturyPhilippineCultureAndSociety)

### 3. Women had documented wartime roles without thereby forming a warrior
class

Loarca describes priestesses and female kin performing sacrifices,
observances, and work restrictions connected with war. These were active and
socially important wartime functions. They are evidence that warfare engaged
the whole community, but ritual participation is not evidence of service in
the fighting line.

**Label:** **Documented**

**Source:** [Loarca (1582)](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/16501/pg16501-images.html)

Barbara Watson Andaya's regional study argues for a wider definition of
participation in warfare, including ritual specialists, camp followers, and
community defence. In discussing the early Spanish conquest of the
Philippines, she cites Felice Noelle Rodríguez's peer-reviewed study for an
episode in which women and children joined men in throwing improvised
weapons. The accessible conquest narrative clearly records women and children
present in contested settlements and records fighting around them, but its
wording does not provide a reliable female combatant count or a formal role.

The responsible conclusion is that direct community defence by women was
possible, not that a routine mixed-gender army existed.

**Label:** **Documented, form uncertain**

**Sources:**

- [Barbara Watson Andaya, "Non-combatants in warfare," in *Women Warriors in
  Southeast Asia*](https://ebrary.net/284383/education/combatants_warfare)
- [Felice Noelle Rodríguez, "Juan de Salcedo Joins the Native Form of
  Warfare," *Journal of the Economic and Social History of the Orient* 46,
  no. 2 (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1163/156852003321675727)
- [Early conquest accounts, in Blair and Robertson, vol. 3](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13616/pg13616-images.html)

## What the evidence does not establish

The sources do not provide:

- a percentage of female combatants;
- a routine female warrior class or female military unit;
- evidence that women normally joined maritime raids or a chief's armed
  following;
- a basis for extrapolating Visayan and Tagalog evidence to Mindanao, Sulu,
  the Cordillera, or every other Philippine society;
- a basis for turning a female ruler, property holder, priestess, or member of
  a privileged social grade automatically into a combatant.

Women may also be under-recorded by male colonial observers. That limitation
makes an absolute "no woman ever fought" conclusion unsafe. It does not turn
silence into positive evidence for a normal mixed-gender force.

Later figures and traditions cannot fill this gap. Urduja is chronologically
and historically unsuitable as proof for the late 1500s; Gabriela Silang and
women in later revolutionary or guerrilla movements belong to different
centuries and military systems.

## Hukbo roster guidance

For an ordinary late-sixteenth-century raid, naval engagement, or assembled
chiefly fighting force:

1. A male combatant roster is the most evidence-backed default.
2. Do not describe that default as proof that women never fought.
3. Do not assign an invented female percentage to create a supposedly
   historical mixed roster.
4. If ordinary female combatants are included for presentation or player
   representation, label the choice **Provisional reconstruction**.
5. A rare female defender in a settlement-defence scenario can be labelled
   **Documented, form uncertain**, provided the presentation does not imply a
   standing female warrior class.
6. A female ritual specialist can be **Documented** as a noncombatant wartime
   role; combat abilities would require a separate reconstruction and design
   decision.

The shortest accurate roster statement is:

> Organized expeditionary warriors were normally men; women had documented
> wartime roles, and direct defensive fighting by women was possible but was
> not a documented normal army composition.

## Source limitations

The strongest near-contemporary descriptions come from Spanish colonial
observers and concentrate on the Visayas and the Tagalog region. Their
categories, translations, and interests can obscure local distinctions. Morga
is retrospective, Scott is a modern synthesis, and Andaya and Rodríguez offer
scholarly interpretations of uneven conquest narratives. This conclusion
should be revised if a more explicit local or near-contemporary account is
identified.
