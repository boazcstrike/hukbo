# Weapon-on-weapon clash in 1500s Philippine and maritime Southeast Asian melee

Research compiled 2026-07-27. Companion to
[HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md](HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md), and bound by the same
evidence-labelling rules: every claim is marked **Documented**, **Documented, form
uncertain**, or **Provisional reconstruction**.

**Scope caution.** "The Philippines in the 1500s" is not one military culture. The usable
evidence clusters around Mactan and Cebu in 1521, Visayan and Luzon encounters between 1565
and 1582, and late-century visual material compiled in Manila. Nothing here should be read
as applying to the whole archipelago or the whole century.

**This is a research document, not a tuning authorization.** Section 5 contains numbers.
Those numbers carry no evidentiary confidence whatsoever — they are game-design choices, in
exactly the sense that `HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` already establishes for the
`PhilippineCombatPreset` target weights and the tall-hardwood shield multiplier.

---

## 0. The headline finding

**No sixteenth-century source describing Philippine combat describes a blade-on-blade
parry.** Not one. The defensive behaviours that are described are evasion by movement and
covering with the shield.

That absence is the most important result of this research, and it matters more than any
number below. It is a real signal rather than merely a gap in the record, because the same
sources describe weapons, armour, shields, missile exchange, and wounds in considerable
detail. If a hard weapon-catch had been the visually dominant event of these fights, a
Spanish observer describing an hour-long melee had every opportunity to say so.

It is not proof that clashes did not happen. Chroniclers describe outcomes and equipment,
not fencing mechanics, and Spanish accounts are evidence about equipment rather than
neutral ethnography. What it does mean is this: **any clash rate in Hukbo is inferred, and
the shield is the only defensive channel with sixteenth-century documentary support.**

## 1. Which defence dominated, by source type

The answer changes completely depending on which body of evidence is consulted, and the
honest reading is that the source types are measuring different things.

### 1.1 Philippine primary sources, 1521 to 1582 — evasion and shield, no parry

**Documented.** Pigafetta's Mactan account of April 1521 is the only near-eyewitness
description of Philippine melee from the period. Its defensive content is entirely evasion
plus shield. Against firearms the islanders would not stand still but leaped about, covering
themselves with their shields — two behaviours in a single clause, footwork and shield
cover. The shields are described as thin wood, and Spanish shot passed through both the
shield and the arm behind it, so these were not intended as arrest-the-blow bucklers. The
offensive side of the account is missile-heavy: arrows, iron-tipped bamboo and rattan
throwing spears, fire-hardened stakes, stones, and finally the closing blade work that
killed Magellan.

**Documented.** Magellan's fatal sequence — a leg wound from a bamboo shaft, a wound to the
arm, then a large cutting sword to the leg — is a wound pattern of penetration, not of
blades being caught and turned.

### 1.2 Later ethnography and epic — the shield as an active deflector

**Documented, form uncertain.** These sources date from the 1600s to the 1900s and are
projected backwards.

Alcina's *Historia de las Islas e Indios de Bisayas* (1668) and Morga's *Sucesos* (1609)
supply the standard equipment picture: long narrow shields, round bucklers, spears, daggers,
long blades, padded and hide armour. Scott's *Barangay* (1994) records the Tagalog round
buckler *palisay* alongside the long narrow *kalasag*, cotton armour, and carabao-hide
breastplates.

Fay-Cooper Cole's 1922 ethnography of Mindanao is the most mechanically specific: shields
were meant to *deflect* missiles rather than actually stop them, and the grip lets the thumb
and fingers tilt the shield to the required angle. That is active, angled deflection, not a
static block. Bagobo fighters are described crouching and dancing backwards behind the
shield, glancing spears and arrows off it. The Panay Bukidnon epic *Hinilawod* has a hero
striking and driving enemies with the shield itself before threatening with the spear.

### 1.3 Filipino Martial Arts technical literature — contact happens, deliberately not edge-on-edge

**Provisional reconstruction for the 1500s.** Living FMA is a nineteenth and twentieth
century codification and cannot be treated as sixteenth-century doctrine. What it supplies
is a coherent internal logic that a blade culture arrives at.

Kalis Ilustrisimo's stated sword doctrine is explicit. Practitioners avoid edge-to-edge
contact for two reasons: it damages the cutting edge, and an edge is an insecure defensive
surface because it tends to slide. Instead the wrist rotates to present the **flat** to the
incoming attack, and the defence is a *brush* that carries the opponent's stroke aside, with
the counter-rotation already primed for the answer.

Note carefully what this means for a simulation. **It is a doctrine about the geometry of
contact, not a doctrine of no contact.** A flat-brush deflection is still a weapon-on-weapon
event. It is quieter, shorter, does not stop the fight, and does not notch the edge — but it
happens.

Alongside it sits "defang the snake": the preferred response to an attack is not to meet the
weapon at all but to attack the hand or forearm holding it, converting the defence into an
offence. And the widely repeated Ilustrisimo position that solidly blocking a committed
blade attack is impractical, with movement, slipping, and re-directive parrying preferred.

### 1.4 HEMA and European material — blade contact common, edge-on-edge avoided

**Documented, European, not Philippine.** The ARMA survey of edge damage assembles the
strongest physical evidence available anywhere for this question. Wallace Collection curator
David Edge reports that heavy edge-gouging is extremely rare on surviving medieval swords.
Hank Reinhardt reads the conspicuous absence of significant edge damage across large numbers
of historical swords as implying deliberate protective practice. Archaeologist Kristian
Kristiansen finds that on Bronze Age blades the area below the hilt — the forte — is the
defensive zone, and it is there that severe damage and repeated resharpening are found, with
transverse ridges consistent with flat-side warding.

That last point is the most useful single fact in this document. **The archaeology says
weapon-on-weapon contact was frequent enough to wear out the forte, and simultaneously says
it was almost never taken on the edge.** Contact was common; edge-block was not.

Joachim Meyer (1570) names edge-parrying, *Auffangen*, and specifically advises against it
as a desperation measure.

European longsword doctrine actively *seeks* the bind, so this source class is an upper
bound and should not be transplanted onto a shield-and-single-blade culture.

### 1.5 Summary

| Source class | Dominant defence described | Weapon-on-weapon parry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Philippine primary, 1521–1582 | Evasion plus shield cover | Unattested |
| Later Philippine ethnography and epic | Active angled shield deflection; shield as weapon | Unattested |
| FMA technical literature | Evade, defang, flat-brush redirect | Present, deliberately soft, never edge-on-edge |
| European archaeology and HEMA | Forte and flat contact; shield or buckler where carried | Frequent, concentrated on the forte |

## 2. Shield primacy and edge preservation

### 2.1 The shield was the defensive instrument of record

**Documented** that shields were universal, large, and central to the equipment picture.
**Documented, form uncertain** on their specific construction and dimensions, since the
object-level detail comes from later collections and later ethnography.

Pigafetta in 1521 has shields present, actively used, thin wood, defeated by Spanish shot.
The Boxer Codex of about 1590 to 1595 depicts large shields carried with spears and blades
across Visayan, Tagalog, Zambal, and Cagayan figures — guiding silhouette and colour, per
repository policy, not exact technical cataloguing. Junker's *Raiding, Trading, and Feasting*
(1999) is cited for rattan-strengthened, resin-coated construction. Krieger (1926, US
National Museum Bulletin 137) documents the pronged Cordilleran forms, whose prongs are
described as functional for trapping and close work rather than decorative.

Secondary summaries give roughly 50 by 150 cm for the large rectangular type and describe
light fibrous wood chosen so that a penetrating spear or dagger becomes *enmeshed* and
cannot be withdrawn. **Flag: this "sword-proof and enmeshing" formulation propagates through
community and AI-generated encyclopedia pages faster than it can be traced to a page in
Scott or Junker.** Treat it as *Documented, form uncertain*, and never cite it as a
measurement.

A light, large, fibrous, resin-and-rattan shield designed to trap and deflect is a
consumable interception surface. That is precisely the object you would build if your plan
is to put the shield between yourself and the blade rather than your own blade.

### 2.2 Evidence that blade-on-blade was avoided to preserve the edge

Two independent lines, neither Philippine.

**Surviving blades (Documented, European).** The ARMA compilation above: heavy edge-gouging
rare on surviving medieval swords, wear and resharpening concentrated on the forte. If
edge-on-edge parry had been routine, museum edges would be sawtoothed. They are not.

**Living doctrine (Documented as doctrine, Provisional as 1500s practice).** Kalis
Ilustrisimo's stated rationale is literally edge preservation plus contact security. This is
the only place in the entire evidence base where a Philippine-lineage tradition states the
reason out loud.

**Metallurgical plausibility (Documented, form uncertain — and the weakest link here).**
Kampilan and kalis-class blades are consistently described as pattern-welded or laminated
constructions folding higher- and lower-carbon layers, giving a hard edge on a tougher body.
A differentially hardened edge on a soft spine is exactly the geometry that chips when
struck square by another hard edge. But no metallurgical study of a dated sixteenth-century
Philippine blade was located. Much of what circulates online about pre-colonial Philippine
metallurgy is enthusiast material with strong nationalist framing and no analytical basis.
**Do not put a hardness number, a chip probability, or a "rivals Toledo" claim in this
repository on this evidence.**

### 2.3 The FMA comparison, stated fairly

FMA live-blade doctrine — check and deflect on the flat, brush rather than block, defang the
snake, no hard edge-to-edge — is *consistent* with the shield-primacy picture and with the
European edge archaeology. Three independent bodies of evidence pointing the same way is
meaningful.

It is not confirmation. FMA as practised is a duelling and self-defence art with a
documented history centuries later than 1521, heavily shaped by rattan-stick training where
edge preservation is irrelevant, and by Spanish-era and modern influences. **The honest
label for "sixteenth-century Visayans fought like Ilustrisimo" is *Provisional
reconstruction*, and this repository must never state it as fact.**

## 3. Weapon pairs and clash likelihood

Everything in this section is **Provisional reconstruction** — reasoning from weapon
geometry and cross-cultural doctrine, not from any Philippine source.

**Heavy chopper versus heavy chopper**, two-handed, no shield, is the highest
weapon-contact class in the roster. There is no shield to absorb the interception, the
weapon is the only defensive instrument other than distance, and both weapons are
forward-heavy with slow recovery, so a committed cut cannot always be voided by footwork
alone. Doctrine says avoid the bind; physics and tempo force contact anyway. Note the
internal tension worth encoding: this is also the pairing where a clash is *most costly*,
because these are the blades whose edges you least want to notch. A simulation that makes
clashes frequent here should also make them consequential here.

**Blade versus shield** is the lowest weapon-on-weapon class. The shield is a larger,
cheaper, more forgiving interception surface positioned closer to the attack line. A
shield-bearer who has the shield up has no reason to spend the blade on the intercept; the
blade is reserved for the answer. The consequence for Hukbo is that a shield should not
merely reduce damage — it should **reroute the defence to a different channel**, which is a
visible, spectator-discoverable difference.

**Blade versus spear** is the most asymmetric pair, and FMA's own range vocabulary —
*largo*, where only weapon tips reach, then *medio*, then *corto* — describes exactly this
problem. The blade side must generate weapon contact to win, because closing on a live spear
point without displacing the shaft is how you get impaled, so the short-weapon fighter's
clash rate should be the highest single figure in the matrix. The spear side should almost
never need to parry, because the correct spear answer to a closing swordsman is retraction
and re-threat rather than catching his blade, so its clash rate should be among the lowest.
**This asymmetry is the most defensible structural claim in the report and should be encoded
before any absolute number is.**

**Spear versus spear** is moderate to high. Two long shafts contesting one centreline
produce shaft-on-shaft beats, sweeps, and binds as a matter of geometry, and staff and spear
traditions everywhere are built around exactly that. Crucially, shaft-on-shaft carries **no
edge-damage cost**, which removes the doctrinal reason to avoid contact.

**Reach differential, generalised.** Reach advantage converts a defensive problem into a
spacing problem. The longer weapon defends by not being there; the shorter weapon defends by
displacing the longer weapon. A single reach-differential modifier should therefore push the
two sides of the same exchange in opposite directions — it is not a symmetric multiplier.

## 4. Quantitative anchors, with their honest distance from the question

There is no quantitative anchor for the actual question. Nobody has measured parry frequency
in sixteenth-century Philippine combat, and nobody has published a per-exchange blade-contact
rate for any historical melee system. What follows is the nearest usable material.

### 4.1 HEMA tournament data — real numbers, wrong variable

From Sword STEM's analysis of the HEMA Scorecard database: 71 longsword tournament events
covering 42,026 exchanges. Double-hit frequency at the 2018 Dutch Lion's Cup was 11.1 to
14.5 per cent, with a rough global estimate across HEMA longsword of about 15 per cent.
Individual fighters averaged 10.7 per cent, mode about 8.5 per cent, worst individual 56 per
cent, over fighters with 30 or more exchanges. Deductive-afterblow rulesets run about 10 to
12 per cent and no-afterblow rulesets above 20 per cent, but the two cannot be pooled because
they classify exchanges differently.

**Confidence high for the number, low for its relevance.** A double hit means both fighters
failed to defend in a scoring exchange. It is a rough floor on defensive failure, not a parry
rate. It also comes from sport longsword with masks, blunt steel, and no shields — a system
that structurally rewards trading in a way a live-blade shield culture does not.

### 4.2 Instrumented sparring — the right method, no usable number

Project Smart Sword instrumented longswords with two ADXL377 accelerometers at roughly 1000
samples per axis per second, using a 25 G threshold and classifying blade-on-blade contact by
accelerometer peaks under 50 ms apart. The authors report they could not reliably separate
sword-to-sword from sword-to-body contact, and published no contact-per-exchange figures.
**Anyone claiming a measured historical parry rate is not working from data.**

### 4.3 Osteology — the strongest indirect evidence, and it points at the limb

| Site | Sample | Figures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visby, 1361 | 1,185 individuals, three graves | 456 cutting-weapon injuries, 126 projectile injuries; head wounds 42.3 / 52.3 / 5.4 per cent by grave |
| Towton, 1461 | 51 in the Towton Hall grave | 28 skulls analysed, 27 with battle injury; 13 individuals with post-cranial wounds |
| Sidon, 1260 | 25 males | 40.0 per cent crude prevalence of sharp-force injury to hand elements |
| Aljubarrota, 1385 | about 400 | 3 individuals with amputated limbs who returned to combat |

The Towton report identifies the **forearms as the primary location of defensive parrying
wounds**, with the right arm leading; Towton 30 shows a peri-mortem parry fracture of the
right ulna. Most wounds fall on the left side, consistent with right-handed attackers.

**Confidence high for the numbers, medium for the inference, and note the direction it
points.** Sidon's 40 per cent hand-injury prevalence and Towton's forearm concentration say
that in real fighting **the limb intercepted the blade very often** — sometimes because the
fighter raised it, sometimes because the hand was the target. This converges with "defang the
snake" from a completely independent direction, and it argues that a model with only
weapon-catch and shield-catch channels is missing the most archaeologically visible
interception surface of all.

Severe survivorship caveat: mass graves are the losing side at the end of a rout. They
over-represent the last thirty seconds of a battle, not the average exchange.

### 4.4 Shield experiments — directional, not numeric

Rolf Warming (University of Copenhagen) tested a reconstructed one-metre pine Viking round
shield across seven shield-and-sword scenarios, varying shield position and active versus
passive posture, while being attacked by a professional instructor. Passive shield use
produced deep cuts and severe damage and failed to prevent a head blow; active, angled use
produced considerably less damage, with the shield functioning almost like a weapon, used to
parry and to strike. The results also explain otherwise-puzzling dents on archaeological
shield bosses.

**Confidence medium-high as physical demonstration, low as a frequency estimate** — the
sample is small and the opponent is cooperating. But note how exactly it matches Cole's 1922
Mindanao description of tilting the shield to angle, and the *Hinilawod* hero striking with
the shield. Three cultures, three centuries apart, same conclusion: **the shield is an
active, angled, aggressive instrument, not a wall.**

### 4.5 Arnis competition — nothing usable

WEKAF full-contact scoring is a ten-point-must system on accumulated clean strikes tracked by
mechanical counters, with a four-second repeat rule, over three rounds. **Blocks are not
scored, therefore not counted, therefore not published.** No parry-rate statistic exists, and
even if it did, padded-stick sport with no edge and no shield says very little about
live-blade behaviour.

## 5. Numeric recommendations

**Every number in this section is a Provisional reconstruction and a gameplay tuning
choice.** None is a historical measurement, none is derived from a statistical study, and
none may ever be cited back into this document or
[HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md](HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md) as evidence.

### 5.1 Model the defence as a channel allocation, not a single probability

The evidence does not support "does a clash happen, yes or no". It supports a defender
choosing *which surface* takes the attack. Two stages:

**Composition rule. Read this before any table below.**

```
P(landed)         = 1 − P(active defence)
P(active defence) = P(shield intercept) + P(weapon intercept) + P(void)
```

The three channels are **mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive of the defence**. They are
never summed on top of a separate base clash probability, and `P(active defence)` is derived
from the channels rather than multiplied against them. Any attacker-side accuracy or whiff
roll, if the simulation models one, is a separate and prior stage and must not be folded in
here or it double-counts.

This rule was missing from the first draft of this document, and its absence caused a real
error: the per-pair table below was read as a free-standing lookup added on top of a 0.20
base, which produced a shielded-defender interception near 0.48 and left the void channel
unaccounted for entirely.

**Stage 1 — does the defender mount any active defence?** For an aware defender, in measure,
unfatigued, in the frontal arc, **facing one attacker**: about 0.50, defensible range 0.35 to
0.65.

That is a duel figure and it is not the battle figure. Hukbo runs a 200-agent mixed melee, and
the modifiers in section 5.4 — ×0.65 per extra attacker in reach with a ×0.25 floor, ×0.10
outside the frontal arc, ×0.70 wounded or fatigued — pull it down substantially at realistic
occupancy. The corrected roster-realistic values are:

| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Duel: aware, one attacker, fresh | 0.50 (0.40–0.60) | 0.38 (0.28–0.48) |
| **Mass melee, roster-realistic** | **0.40 (0.32–0.47)** | **0.25 (0.18–0.32)** |

### 5.2 Aggregate target for the Hukbo roster

The shipped roster is four melee loadouts in equal counts: `GreatBlade` and `HeavyChopper`
shieldless, `ThrustingBlade` and `Bolo` carrying `TallHardwood`. Half the army is shielded,
none of it carries a spear, and there are no missile troops. It therefore cannot inherit a
generic average from any of the source material.

Working the section 5.3 matrix through at equal loadout weights, each loadout supplying a
quarter of attacks and a quarter of defences:

| Defender loadout | Mean shield intercept | Mean weapon intercept | Void | **Total non-landed** |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| GreatBlade | 0.00 | 0.19 | 0.10 | **0.29** |
| HeavyChopper | 0.00 | 0.13 | 0.09 | **0.22** |
| ThrustingBlade + TallHardwood | 0.24 | 0.05 | 0.10 | **0.39** |
| Bolo + TallHardwood | 0.25 | 0.04 | 0.11 | **0.40** |
| **Roster weighted mean** | 0.12 | 0.10 | 0.10 | **0.33** |

**Acceptance criterion one — interception share.** Defence-attributable non-landed share
measured over a whole 200-agent battle: target **0.30 to 0.40, centre 0.33; fail outside 0.25
to 0.45.** Measure shield intercepts plus weapon intercepts plus voids, divided by accepted
attack attempts against a defender in reach. Do not measure total non-landed including
attacker accuracy failure — that band depends on a system the clash model does not own, and
mixing them produces a criterion that moves when unrelated code changes.

**Acceptance criterion two — termination.** An interception band alone cannot catch a stall,
because time to decision is proportional to `1 / (attack rate × (1 − intercept) × damage per
landed blow)`. Moving interception from 0.48 to 0.33 shortens time to decision by a factor of
roughly 1.29 — a 29 per cent improvement, not a fix. If a battle stalls at 0.48 it may still
stall at 0.33. So: **across a sweep of at least 20 seeds at 200 agents, at least 95 per cent
of battles must reach a winner before the tick cap, and the *median* decisive tick must be at
or below 50 per cent of the cap.** The median clause is the one that catches the stall; a
95-per-cent-termination clause alone passes happily while every battle finishes at 98 per
cent of the cap. Both clauses are required.

#### 5.3.1 What drives the ordering, and where it is unsupported

Four structural claims produce every cell, and only the first two have any anchor.

**A shield roughly doubles total defence and reroutes it — weak analogical anchor.** Pigafetta
1521 records shields actively interposed (*Documented*, but against missiles rather than
blades); Cole 1922 describes an angled, tilted, deflecting use with a grip built for it
(*Documented, form uncertain*, three centuries late); Warming's Trelleborg trials show active
shield use sharply reduces damage relative to passive (*Documented as experiment*, Viking, not
Philippine). None of that yields a rate. The direction is anchored; the magnitude is invented.

**A long two-handed blade parries better than a forward-weighted chopper — weak anchor.** The
European archaeology puts defensive wear on the forte, the leverage-rich zone near the hilt. A
weapon with its mass thrown forward has less usable forte and slower recovery. This is
mechanics, and European mechanics at that. It is why the GreatBlade defender sits at 0.22 and
the HeavyChopper defender at 0.15.

**Thrusts are harder to intercept with a weapon than cuts — pure extrapolation.** Carried over
from the ×0.70 thrust modifier in section 5.4, itself inferred from line geometry and
commitment timing. Note the asymmetry deliberately encoded: a thrust is harder for a *blade*
to catch but not meaningfully harder for a *tall shield* to cover, because a shield defends an
area while a blade defends a line. The `ThrustingBlade` attacker column therefore depresses
the weapon table without depressing the shield table. No source supports this.

**A short blade cannot arrest a heavy one — pure extrapolation.** `Bolo` and `ThrustingBlade`
defenders sit at 0.03 to 0.06 weapon intercept against the heavies. Mass-mismatch reasoning
plus the FMA doctrinal position that solidly blocking a committed attack is impractical
(*Documented as modern doctrine, Provisional for the 1500s*). It also produces the intended
design consequence: for the shielded loadouts the blade is almost purely offensive, which is a
clean and spectator-legible role separation.

**Cells with no anchor whatsoever:** the entire `ThrustingBlade` and `Bolo` weapon-intercept
rows (eight cells — no source describes a narrow thruster or a short work blade parrying
anything); every `ThrustingBlade` attacker column (four cells); and all within-table variation
of the shield rows (eight cells, spread only 0.22 to 0.27, which is honest signalling that
there is no basis for claiming a `TallHardwood` shield performs differently against a
`GreatBlade` than against a `Bolo`). That is sixteen of sixteen cells with no evidentiary
confidence. The ordering is arguable; the numbers are not.

| Attack is a heavy committed cut | ×1.15 | 1.05–1.30 | Long, readable arc |

| Defender engaged by two or more attackers in reach | ×0.65 per extra attacker, floor ×0.25 | — | Cannot cover every line |
| Defender wounded or fatigued | ×0.70 | 0.60–0.85 | — |
| Elite or veteran defender | ×1.30 | 1.15–1.45 | Skill buys tempo to intercept |
| Levy or unskilled defender | ×0.70 | 0.55–0.85 | — |

### 5.5 Two design recommendations that follow from the evidence rather than the numbers

**Add a limb-interception channel.** The best-evidenced interception surface in the entire
quantitative record is neither the shield nor the weapon — it is the forearm and hand
(Sidon's 40 per cent hand-injury prevalence, Towton's forearms as the primary defensive-wound
site). This converges independently with FMA's defang-the-snake targeting. Suggested 0.06 to
0.12 of exchanges resolving as a limb intercept, producing a hand or forearm hit location,
which the existing hit-location system can already express.

**Split clash into hard and soft.** The doctrine evidence is unanimous that hard
weapon-arresting contact is avoided while soft redirecting contact is normal, and a single
"clash" event flattens the most interesting thing in the sources.

A flat split is too coarse for this roster: the hard share varies by roughly sixfold across
the matrix, and flattening it discards the most legible contrast the system can express.
**This axis applies to the weapon channel only** — a shield intercept is not a parry and gets
its own split below.

Base hard share, driven by the **incoming** weapon:

| Incoming attack | Hard share | Band |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| HeavyChopper | 0.40 | 0.28–0.52 |
| GreatBlade | 0.33 | 0.22–0.45 |
| Bolo | 0.18 | 0.10–0.28 |
| ThrustingBlade | 0.12 | 0.06–0.20 |

Multiplier by the **defending** instrument: `GreatBlade` ×1.15 (two hands and a long forte can
afford to arrest), `HeavyChopper` ×1.05 (mass but poor leverage), `ThrustingBlade` ×0.75
(cannot arrest, redirects or fails), `Bolo` ×0.70 (shortest, least leverage). Clamp the
product to 0.05–0.60.

The two extremes: a `GreatBlade` defending against a `HeavyChopper` — both heavy, both
shieldless — gives **0.46 (0.32–0.60)**, the highest in the roster, the heaviest incoming
weapon meeting the only instrument capable of arresting it. This is the edge-destroying
pairing, and it also carries the highest weapon-intercept rate, so clashes there are both
frequent and costly. A `Bolo` defending against a `ThrustingBlade` — both light, both shielded
— gives **0.08 (0.04–0.15)**, roughly six times lower, and is rare regardless: weapon
intercept in that cell is only 0.05, so hard clashes occur in about 0.4 per cent of those
exchanges.

Because most weapon intercepts come from the shieldless heavies, the **roster-weighted hard
share is about 0.35 (0.25–0.45)** — roughly 35/65, replacing the earlier flat 25/75.

**Shield intercept mode, a separate split.** Angled deflection, active: 0.65 (0.55–0.75), no
shield degradation. Flat take, passive or forced: 0.35 (0.25–0.45), degrades. The direction is
anchored to Warming's finding that passive use produced deep cuts and severe damage while
active angled use produced considerably less, and to Cole's tilting grip; the split value
itself is invented.

Only hard clashes should ever carry an edge-degradation consequence, and only on edged
weapons. Note that **all four shipped loadouts are edged, so this roster has no free-contact
weapon class anywhere** — unlike a spear-inclusive roster, where shaft-on-shaft carries no
edge cost and can afford to clash far more freely.

If edge degradation is ever implemented, 5 to 15 per cent chance of a notch per hard edged
clash is a defensible starting band. There is no metallurgical study of a dated
sixteenth-century Philippine blade to anchor it, so it must be marked `PROVISIONAL` in source
and in tests exactly as the tall-hardwood shield multiplier already is.

### 5.6 Spectator discoverability

Per `SIMULATION-GAME-STANDARDS.md` §10, a clash system is only complete if a spectator can
discover it without reading source. Channel-splitting is the feature that makes that
possible: "his shield took it", "he turned it aside with the blade", "he stepped off the
line", and "it caught his forearm" are four visually and audibly distinguishable outcomes. A
single scalar clash probability is not discoverable and should not ship.

### 5.7 Sanity bounds, and one structural problem the bounds cannot fix

**Upper bound, from the bones.** Sidon 1260 records 40.0 per cent crude prevalence of
sharp-force injury to hand elements across 25 males, with one individual carrying a minimum of
twelve injuries across sixteen skeletal elements. Towton 1461 has 27 of 28 analysed skulls
showing battle injury and one individual with nine wounds. Visby 1361 records 456
cutting-weapon injuries across the assemblage. All *Documented*. The inference: once a man was
engaged and losing, blows landed on him in rapid succession. Nine wounds on one skeleton and
twelve on another are not the output of a system where every second blow is turned aside.

The caveat cuts both ways. Mass graves are the losing side at the end of a rout, so they
over-represent the final seconds and describe the tail of the distribution rather than its
centre. They bound the model from above without pinning it. **Aggregate active defence above
about 0.55 is indefensible** — past that, more blows are stopped than land, and a mass melee
becomes a mutual-blocking stalemate that no account and no assemblage describes.

**Lower bound, from the equipment record.** Every source in the corpus shows shields
universally present and evidently worth the weight, the wood, the rattan, and the resin;
Junker's chiefdom-economy framing makes them a real production cost. If a shield does not
visibly change outcomes, the entire equipment record is inexplicable. **Aggregate active
defence below about 0.15 is indefensible**, and separately the shielded-versus-shieldless gap
must stay clearly visible. The 0.40 against 0.25 in section 5.1 is the part to defend hardest,
above any absolute value.

**Mactan as a duration anchor, and why not to lean on it.** *Documented:* Pigafetta gives
internal time markers — musketry and crossbow fire for about half an hour, then a prolonged
closing fight — supporting an engagement on the order of an hour, decisively resolved. But
Mactan was roughly 49 Spaniards against a very large local force, mostly resolved by missile
exchange and an opposed withdrawal across water. It is a wildly asymmetric amphibious action,
not a symmetric mass melee. Using "Mactan lasted an hour" to tune a 200-agent balanced-roster
attrition model generalises one engagement to a battle shape it does not describe — the exact
failure this document's scope caution warns against. Use it for texture, not for a tick target.

**The structural problem.** Pre-modern battles were decided by morale collapse and rout, not
by attrition to exhaustion. Winners typically took light casualties; losers took most of
theirs during the rout, after cohesion broke. The killing followed the decision rather than
producing it.

Hukbo has no morale model, and `CLAUDE.md` §9 correctly defers one. **Hukbo must therefore
reach a decision purely by attrition — by a mechanism that historically did not decide
battles.** The consequence is unavoidable and belongs where a future reader will find it:
Hukbo's interception rate has to be tuned below whatever the historical record would suggest,
or Hukbo will produce a battle shape that never occurred. Not because the history is wrong,
but because the simulation is missing the mechanism that actually ended battles, and attrition
has to do that work in its place.

**That is a design compensation and explicitly not a historical claim. It must never be
laundered back into this document, or into `HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md`, as evidence about
how often people parried.**

| Aggregate active-defence share | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| Below 0.15 | **Indefensible.** Shields become pointless; contradicts the universal equipment record. |
| 0.15 to 0.25 | Low but arguable, as explicit compensation for the absent morale model. Label it as such. |
| **0.30 to 0.40** | **Recommended operating band. Centre 0.33.** |
| 0.45 to 0.55 | Upper margin. Only defensible if the model gains morale or rout. |
| Above 0.55 | **Indefensible.** Cannot produce the Sidon and Towton wound counts in the time available. |

## 8. Gaps a follow-up should close

1. **Scott's *Barangay* was never read at page level.** The full text is on archive.org but
   could not be extracted here. Its warfare chapter is the single highest-value unread source
   for this question, and the "sword-proof, enmeshing kalasag" formulation needs to be traced
   to a page or dropped.
2. **No metallurgical study of a dated sixteenth-century Philippine blade was found.** Until
   one is, no hardness, chip-rate, or edge-durability figure belongs in code or comments
   except as `PROVISIONAL`.
3. **Fencing notational analysis** giving parry-riposte action shares likely exists in
   sports-science databases unreachable from this environment.
4. **Two PDFs — the Towton osteological analysis and the Combat Archaeology shield article —
   could not be text-extracted.** Their figures came via secondary summary and should be
   verified directly before anyone cites exact counts.
