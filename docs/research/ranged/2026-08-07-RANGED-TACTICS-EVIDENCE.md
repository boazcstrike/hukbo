# Ranged Tactics and Missile Behavior in Sixteenth-Century Philippine Warfare

Research date: 2026-08-07

Purpose: establish what the surviving record actually says about how missile
weapons were *used* in pre-colonial and early-contact Philippine land warfare
around the 1500s — the sequence of a fight, where a missile-armed man stood,
whether anyone shot together, how long two sides stayed apart, and what happened
when a bowman was reached by a man with a blade. The companion document
[2026-08-07-RANGED-WEAPONS-EVIDENCE.md](2026-08-07-RANGED-WEAPONS-EVIDENCE.md)
covers the weapons themselves; this one covers behavior.

This is a research reference for Hukbo's battle layer. It does not define
gameplay statistics, balance values, or final reconstructions, and nothing in it
authorizes implementation work. Every claim carries one of the three confidence
labels required by
[`CLAUDE.md`](../../../CLAUDE.md) section 7 and defined in
[`HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md`](../HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md).

## 1. Research boundary and confidence labels

The same boundary that governs
[`HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md`](../HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md) and
[`ARMY-COMPOSITION.md`](../ARMY-COMPOSITION.md) governs this document, and it
bites harder here than it does for equipment. A weapon can be described from a
single glance; a tactic can only be described by someone who watched a fight
from beginning to end and understood what he was seeing. Almost nobody in this
corpus did both.

The evidence used here is:

| Window | Place | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1521 | Mactan, Cebu, Palawan, Cagayan Sulu | Pigafetta's account of the Magellan voyage |
| 1543 | Sarangani and the Mindanao coast | The Villalobos expedition accounts, as summarised by Scott |
| 1565-1567 | Cebu, Bohol, the Visayas | Legazpi's own relations and letters |
| 1569-1572 | Mindoro, Bombon (Taal), Manila, Lubang, Bangkusay, Cainta, Pampanga | The anonymous 1569 relation, the 1570 relation of the voyage to Luzon, and the 1572 "Conquest of the Island of Luzon" |
| 1574-1575 | Manila and Pangasinan | Sande's relation of the Limahong campaign |
| 1577-1579 | Borneo, Jolo, the Rio Grande de Mindanao | Sande's expedition papers and the Ribera fleet's notarised testimony |
| 1582 | The Visayas | Loarca's *Relacion de las Yslas Filipinas* |
| 1609 (retrospective) | Luzon and the Visayas | Morga's *Sucesos de las Islas Filipinas* |
| 1994 (synthesis) | Archipelago-wide | William Henry Scott, *Barangay*, chapter 8, "Weapons and War", and the regional chapters |

Four distortions matter more for tactics than they do for equipment, and every
claim below should be read through them.

1. **Every eyewitness was on the other side, and most of them were shooting
   back.** There is no Philippine account of a Philippine battle from this
   period. What survives is a description of what it looked like to be shot at
   by Filipinos, written by the man being shot at, usually shortly after he
   won. A Spaniard who reports that the enemy "could not endure the firing"
   is reporting an outcome he caused and is proud of.
2. **The Spanish saw almost no Filipino-versus-Filipino battles.** Nearly every
   engagement in the corpus is asymmetric: matchlocks, steel, and mail against
   bows, bamboo, and cotton armor. Behavior that was a rational response to
   arquebuses is not evidence of how the same people fought each other.
3. **The translation is doing tactical work.** The Blair and Robertson English
   frequently says "volley" where the Spanish almost certainly said something
   closer to a shower or sprinkling of arrows. An English military reader hears
   a commanded, simultaneous discharge in that word. Nothing in the underlying
   texts establishes one, and section 5 is entirely about this problem.
4. **Scott's *Barangay* is a synthesis, not a source.** It is by far the best
   guide to this material and it is used heavily below, but it compresses
   sixteenth- and seventeenth-century evidence — Alcina (1668), Mentrida (1637),
   Lisboa (1628) — into single sentences. Where a claim in this document rests
   on Scott's seventeenth-century vocabulary evidence rather than on a
   sixteenth-century eyewitness, it is said so explicitly, because the
   hundred-year attestation rule in [`CLAUDE.md`](../../../CLAUDE.md) section 7
   applies to a tactic exactly as it applies to a weapon name.

### Confidence labels

These are the three labels required by the historical accuracy policy, read for
behavior rather than for objects:

- **Documented** — a sixteenth-century participant describes the behavior
  happening, in a named place, at a named time.
- **Documented, form uncertain** — the behavior is attested, but its extent, its
  regularity, or whether it was deliberate rather than incidental is uncertain.
- **Provisional reconstruction** — a plausible reading supported by later
  vocabulary, by comparative Southeast Asian practice, or by inference from
  documented behavior, but not established for any cited event.

A fourth outcome is used where it is the honest one: **not supported**, for a
behavior that this pass looked for specifically and did not find.

## 2. The headline finding, stated first

The single most important result of this pass, and the one the rest of the
document exists to support, is this:

**In the surviving record, Philippine missile fighting is individual,
opportunistic, evasive, and mostly not decisive. Massed volley fire on command
is not attested anywhere. The strongest evidence for concentrated missile
shooting comes from boat fighting, not from land battle. And a very large share
of the missile evidence describes harassment from cover, across water, or from
behind a fortification — not a missile phase of a pitched field battle, because
pitched field battle is itself close to absent from the record.**

Six supporting findings, each expanded in its own section below:

1. There *is* a documented sequence in which missiles come before contact, but
   in every well-described case the missile stage is either the whole fight or
   ends because one side breaks, not because two lines of infantry collide
   (section 3).
2. No source describes a missile screen falling back through or around a main
   body. The only explicit description of skirmishers in the entire corpus is of
   a **Chinese** pirate force at Manila in 1574, and it is regularly
   misattributed to Filipinos in modern writing (sections 4 and 14).
3. Shooting is described as continuous, hurried, and individually aimed —
   "shooting arrows as rapidly as they could", "the natives shot only at our
   legs" — never as timed or commanded (section 5).
4. Standoffs of half an hour to three hours are documented, but every one of
   them occurs across an obstacle the attacker could not immediately cross:
   surf, a river, a water-filled moat, a cliff (section 6).
5. Archers were reported "in most places but in small numbers", and the bow is
   explicitly a regional rather than a general weapon; the sources give no
   ratio and no roster (section 8).
6. The arquebus in local hands is documented, rare, boat-carried, and judged by
   Legazpi himself to be carried "more to terrify than to kill" (section 10).

Everything that follows either supports one of those six or records what could
not be found.
## 3. Was there a distinct missile phase before melee closed?

**Answer: yes, a missile stage before contact is documented — but "before melee
closed" assumes a closure that usually did not happen.**

### The Mactan evidence, 27 April 1521

Mactan is the most detailed combat account in the entire corpus and it is worth
quoting at length, because almost every question in this document is answered
somewhere in these few paragraphs. Pigafetta, who was present and wounded,
describes the shape of the fight
([Blair and Robertson, Volume XXXIII](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/42884)):

> When we reached land, those men had formed in three divisions to the number
> of more than one thousand five hundred persons. When they saw us, they
> charged down upon us with exceeding loud cries, two divisions on our flanks
> and the other on our front. When the captain saw that, he formed us into two
> divisions, and thus did we begin to fight. The musketeers and crossbowmen
> shot from a distance for about a half-hour, but uselessly; for the shots only
> passed through the shields which were made of thin wood and the arms [of the
> bearers].

And then the Filipino side of the exchange:

> They shot so many arrows at us and hurled so many bamboo spears (some of them
> tipped with iron) at the captain-general, besides pointed stakes hardened
> with fire, stones, and mud, that we could scarcely defend ourselves.

The engagement ran "more than one hour" after that, fought in knee-deep water,
and ended with Magellan speared in the face, wounded in the arm, cut in the leg
with "a large cutlass, which resembles a scimitar, only being larger", and
killed. Fifteen of the islanders died; eight Spaniards and four allied Cebuano
Christians died.

Read for sequence, Mactan gives:

- **A missile stage lasting roughly half an hour, then continuing.** The
  Spanish shot first, ineffectively, for about half an hour. The islanders'
  missile fire was not a preliminary bombardment that stopped when contact
  came — it continued throughout, and it is what drove the Spanish back into
  the water.
- **Missiles doing the tactical work and blades doing the killing.** The
  arrows, spears, stakes, stones, and mud broke the Spanish formation and
  wounded Magellan in the leg. The men who actually killed him closed with a
  bamboo spear, then a cutlass, and only after he was already wounded, isolated,
  and surrounded. **Documented**: missiles produced the decision, edged weapons
  executed it.
- **No moment of line-versus-line collision.** There is no passage in which two
  bodies of men close and fight along a front. What happens instead is that the
  Spanish retire, the islanders follow, and men are killed individually as they
  are caught.

The Mactan sequence is therefore **Documented** as: form up in divisions →
close with shouting → sustained mutual missile exchange at short range →
pursuit and individual close killing of a broken enemy. **Documented, form
uncertain**: whether that sequence is a Philippine tactical norm or a response
to fifty armored strangers wading ashore through surf.

### Every other engagement follows the same shape

The pattern recurs everywhere in the corpus with a missile opening and a
collapse rather than a clash:

- **Bombon (Taal), 1570.** Spanish troops landed and advanced on foot. "The
  Moros ... were shooting arrows as rapidly as they could, and wildly shouting.
  The Moros waited until the Spaniards began to hit their flanks with arquebuse
  bullets; and then, seeing the rage of their opponents, they took to flight"
  ([Volume III](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/13616)). Missile opening; no
  contact; rout. **Documented.**
- **Bangkusay, 3 June 1571.** Twenty to thirty boats came out "and with a great
  outcry began to fire their culverins and many arrows ... When they were in
  close proximity, all the arquebusiers began to shoot and to cause injuries
  among the enemy — who, not being able to endure the firing, which killed many
  of them, began to turn their backs and retreat to the land." Missile opening;
  no boarding; rout. **Documented.**
- **Manila, 1570.** The palisade was taken in "the twinkling of an eye" and the
  bombardiers thrown down with their linstocks still in hand. There is no
  missile phase at all on the Spanish side of the account, because the Spanish
  did not give one.
- **Lubang, 1570.** The defenders "began to discharge their culverins and a few
  arrows" and the resulting "skirmish lasted in one place or the other about
  three hours" without either side reaching the other, because a water-filled
  moat lay between them. This is discussed in section 6.

Taken together, the corpus is **Documented** for a missile stage preceding
contact and **not supported** for a missile stage that ends in a mutual infantry
collision. In the accounts that survive, the fight ends when someone runs.

## 4. Skirmishers: was there a screen, and did it withdraw?

**Answer: not supported. No source in this corpus describes a Philippine missile
screen deployed forward of a main body, and none describes one withdrawing
through or around anything.**

This was searched for directly, and the negative result is firm enough to be
worth stating as a finding rather than a gap.

### What is actually there instead

Three attested patterns look superficially like skirmishing and are not:

1. **Concealed harassers who never had a main body behind them.** At Bombon in
   1570 Salcedo's *prau* was shot at "suddenly, and without them being able to
   see any one" from a fortified position on both banks of a narrow water. On
   the Rio Grande de Mindanao in March 1579 the Spaniards were shot at "all
   along the river, both by day and night", and the party responsible turned
   out on interrogation to be **fourteen men** — "some of them were timaguas,
   and others slaves belonging to Limasancay" — sent from Buayen two days
   earlier in *barotos*
   ([Volume IV](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/12635)). Fourteen men in
   canoes shooting across a river is a raiding detachment, not a screen.
   **Documented.**
2. **Archers on and behind fortifications.** The 1569 relation says of the
   islanders generally that "they are accustomed to fortify themselves in
   strong positions, where they mount their artillery and archery, surrounding
   them outside with ditches full of water". In Bikol, Scott records *bantayan*
   (his text prints *bantam*), "a bamboo tower for archers armed with bows
   (*sikarom*) so long they had to be held under the foot to put the bowstring
   on" — though that vocabulary comes from Lisboa's 1628 dictionary, a century
   after the depicted period. **Documented** for archers on fortifications;
   **Documented, form uncertain** for the Bikol archer tower specifically.
3. **Allied specialist archer contingents.** Scott records that Visayan archers'
   "lack of skill was regularly contrasted with the expertise of mountain
   Negritos, or Sangil islanders who sent contingents of bowmen to reinforce
   their Mindanao allies when needed", and quotes Rada in 1577: "when we were
   taking the field against the corsair Limahong, a chieftain of the Zambals
   came to us with 100 archers, saying he wanted to go to war with us, and that
   he wanted as his whole prize no more than simply the Chinese heads." This is
   the closest thing in the record to a dedicated body of missile troops — and
   it is an **allied contingent under its own chief, negotiating its own price
   in heads**, not a screening element of somebody else's army. **Documented.**

### The skirmisher passage that is not Filipino

Modern writing on this subject repeatedly attributes a skirmisher formation to
Philippine forces on the strength of a passage in Sande's 1575 relation of the
Limahong campaign. The passage exists, and it describes the **Chinese** landing
force, not Filipinos:

> in the boats there were seven hundred men, among whom were a few
> arquebusiers, and many pikemen, besides men armed with battle-axes ... Their
> manner of warfare or of fighting, was to form a squadron composed of men with
> battle-axes, among whom were placed some arquebusiers, a few of the latter
> going ahead as skirmishers. One of every ten men carried a banner, fastened
> to his shoulders and reaching two palms above his head.

The Zambals appear a few paragraphs earlier and are described only as a people
"accustomed to the use of bows and arrows" who cut off heads. The banners, the
squadron, the axe-and-arquebus mix, and the forward skirmishers all belong to
Limahong's force. See section 14 for how this misattribution travels. **This is
a source-criticism finding, not a tactical one, and it is the single most
important correction in this document.**

## 5. Volley fire, or individual and opportunistic shooting?

**This is the question the game most needs answered, so it gets the longest
treatment and the most cautious verdict.**

**Answer: individual and opportunistic. Massed, commanded, simultaneous volley
fire is not attested anywhere in this corpus. The English word "volley" appears
in the translations and does not carry its drill meaning.**

### What the sources actually describe

Every description of Philippine missile shooting in the corpus that says
anything about *how* it was done describes continuous, hurried, individually
directed shooting:

- **"Shooting arrows as rapidly as they could, and wildly shouting"** — Bombon,
  1570. Rate of fire limited by the individual, accompanied by noise, not by
  timing. **Documented.**
- **"They shot so many arrows at us and hurled so many bamboo spears ...
  besides pointed stakes hardened with fire, stones, and mud"** — Mactan, 1521.
  A mixed, simultaneous, individually improvised shower of everything to hand,
  including mud. Nothing about this is a controlled discharge. **Documented.**
- **"The natives shot only at our legs, for the latter were bare"** — Mactan,
  1521. This is the strongest single line in the corpus on this question. Aimed
  fire at an observed weak point in an individual target's protection is
  incompatible with a commanded volley at a body of men. **Documented.**
- **"Picking up the same spear four or six times, hurled it at us again and
  again"** — Mactan, 1521. Ammunition was recovered and reused mid-fight. A
  volley system requires a magazine; this describes a scavenging economy.
  **Documented.**
- **"Native bowmen who ... are good marksmen in the extreme"** — Sarangani,
  1543, where the defenders killed fifty of Villalobos's men over three months
  of contested harvest. Marksmanship, sustained over months, against individual
  targets. **Documented.**
- **"These fine spears were thrown only where it was possible to retrieve
  them — in face-to-face combat, for instance, or from ambush"** — Scott's
  synthesis. The good weapons were thrown only when the thrower expected to
  walk over and pick them up. That is an individual economic calculation made
  per throw. **Documented, form uncertain**, since it is Scott's reading of the
  Visayan vocabulary and of Alcina.

### The "volley" problem

The Blair and Robertson translations use "volley" in at least four places for
Philippine missile fire: "prevented the Moros from discharging another volley
of arrows" (Bombon 1570), "they sent a volley of arrows toward his Grace"
(Tapaca 1579), "a volley of poisoned arrows was discharged from the other side
of the river" and "sent another volley of arrows" (Mindanao 1579). Scott, too,
writes that some Visayans "used arrows to good effect in naval warfare, pouring
volleys into enemy ships at close quarters", and cites the Visayan expression
*"Baga nauran nga odyong"* — "raining like arrows".

None of this establishes drill. Three reasons:

1. **The word is the translators' and Scott's, not the witnesses'.** These are
   early-twentieth-century English renderings of sixteenth-century Spanish, in
   which a shower or sprinkling of arrows is the ordinary idiom. The repository
   already carries the caution that "the Blair and Robertson translation is
   itself an interpretation"
   ([`HISTORICAL_1500s_RANKS.md`](../HISTORICAL_1500s_RANKS.md), "Gaps and
   unknowns"), and this is the clearest case in the whole corpus where a single
   English word could manufacture a tactical system.
2. **The one place the word is unpacked, it means fourteen men shooting from
   cover.** The Mindanao 1579 "volley" is the only episode where the source
   goes on to tell us who fired it. The answer is fourteen men in canoes,
   shooting repeatedly, refusing to stop when shot at, and destroyed when six
   soldiers crossed the river. That is a raiding party keeping up a fire, not a
   rank discharging on command.
3. **The corpus is explicit that there was no command apparatus for it.**
   [`ARMY-COMPOSITION.md`](../ARMY-COMPOSITION.md) §7 records the finding that
   nothing in the sixteenth-century record establishes "a shouted command
   vocabulary, a signal code by horn, gong, drum, or flag, a messenger
   organization, a reserve, a rule for replacing a fallen leader". Volley fire
   is a command technology before it is a shooting technology. A force with no
   attested command signal cannot be asserted to have had one.

### The strongest counter-evidence, and what it is worth

Two pieces of evidence pull the other way and must be recorded honestly.

**The karakoa fighting deck.** Scott, on Visayan naval combat: "From the
fighting decks of karakoa cruisers, 30-centimeter-long spikes or javelins of
heavy hardwood pointed at both ends were thrown in large numbers with an
accuracy which attracted the attention of all foreign observers." Morga
independently describes the platform that made this possible — a bamboo gangway
above the rowers "upon which the fighting-men stand, in order not to interfere
with the rowing of the oarsmen"
([Volume XVI](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15157)). A row of men on a raised
platform throwing "in large numbers" is genuinely concentrated missile fire.
**Documented** as concentrated; **not supported** as simultaneous or commanded.
And it is naval — see section 9.

**The Mactan three divisions.** Fifteen hundred men in three named divisions,
two enveloping the flanks and one holding the front, is real tactical
articulation and is the only formation described in the entire corpus. But
Pigafetta says nothing whatever about how those divisions shot. He describes
their movement, their shouting, and the missiles that came out of them, and
never a moment of collective timing.

### Verdict for the game

**Individual, opportunistic, target-of-opportunity shooting is the historically
defensible model, and it is defensible at the "Documented" tier.** A synchronised
volley mechanic would be a **game-design invention** and would have to be
labelled as one wherever a spectator can see it. Concentrated fire from a fixed
platform or a fortification is defensible as an emergent consequence of many
individuals shooting from the same place at the same target, which is a very
different implementation from a commanded volley and produces a visibly
different picture on screen.
## 6. Standoff: did sides exchange missiles without closing, and for how long?

**Answer: yes, and for a long time — but in every documented case something
physical stopped the two sides from reaching each other. Where nothing did, the
fight resolved in minutes.**

This section exists because Hukbo currently produces long standoff draws, and
the question was asked in the honest form: is that historically plausible, or is
it a bug wearing a costume? The answer is genuinely split, and the split is the
finding.

### Documented standoffs, with their durations and their causes

| Engagement | Duration of missile-only exchange | What prevented closure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mactan, 1521 | About half an hour of Spanish fire, then a further hour-plus of mutual missile fighting | Rocks and shoal water; the Spanish waded "more than two crossbow flights" and fought "up to our knees in the water" |
| Lubang, 1570 | "The skirmish lasted in one place or the other about three hours" | A water-filled ditch two and a half *brazas* deep around the fort; the Spaniards "could not assault or enter the fort because of the moat" |
| Lubang, second fort, 1570 | An entire day of mutual fire — "during the whole day we were not able to enter the fort" | The same, plus heat, hunger, and Spanish numbers |
| Rio Grande de Mindanao, 1579 | Repeated exchanges "all along the river, both by day and night" over days | The river, and the fact that the archers arrived and left by canoe |
| Tapaca, 1579 | Repeated arrow and culverin discharges at the fleet during a parley | The river; the Spanish were on the water and the shooters on the far bank |
| Sarangani, 1543 | Three months of attrition costing fifty Spanish lives | The Spaniards were harvesting a field; the bowmen lived on higher ground and never had to accept contact |

All **Documented**.

### Documented non-standoffs, and how fast they ended

| Engagement | What happened | Elapsed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bombon (Taal), 1570, on the open plain | Arrows "as rapidly as they could", then flight the moment arquebus balls struck their flanks | One exchange |
| Bangkusay, 1571 | Culverins and "many arrows" at range; when the Spanish boats closed, the line broke and fled to land | One approach |
| Manila, 1570 | Palisade stormed "in the twinkling of an eye" | No missile phase at all |
| Cainta, 1572 | After the Spaniards were inside, "the enemy made no resistance"; whoever could flee, fled | Immediate |

All **Documented**.

### What this means, stated plainly

The evidence supports a very specific and rather uncomfortable model:

- **A long missile standoff is historically plausible when, and only when, an
  obstacle or a refusal makes contact optional.** Water, a moat, a cliff, a
  palisade, or simply the freedom to stand on higher ground and shoot at people
  in a field. Under those conditions, hours or months of missile exchange are
  **Documented**.
- **A long missile standoff between two forces standing on the same open ground
  with nothing between them is not documented anywhere in this corpus.** Every
  time the corpus puts two bodies on open ground within missile range, one of
  them breaks almost immediately.
- **The third documented option is to not be there at all.** Scott records that
  the ordinary response to a raid was evacuation: "The best defense against
  *mangayaw* attack was to intercept the enemy at sea, but most communities did
  not have the men-o'-war to do so. They would therefore abandon their homes to
  looting and the torch, and withdraw to the hills." Declining battle was
  normal, not shameful. **Documented.**

For Hukbo, the honest reading is that **a standoff is not automatically
unhistorical, but a standoff between two equal forces on flat open ground with
no obstacle, no terrain, no evacuation option, and no exhaustion of ammunition
is a simulation artifact, not a historical picture.** The historical standoffs
all have a *reason* in the world; the game's do not, because the game has no
terrain, no ammunition, and nowhere to withdraw to. That is a design finding,
not a licence.

Note also what the corpus never shows: a standoff ending in a draw. The
documented standoffs end when the attacker finds a boat and crosses the moat,
when six soldiers wade the river, when the harvest is finally in, or when the
defenders sue for peace and hand over a hundred *taels* of gold. Somebody always
resolves it, and usually by physically closing.

## 7. Ambush, terrain, and the raid-versus-battle balance

**Answer: ambush is the best-attested Philippine tactic in the entire record,
and pitched field battle is close to absent. This is a finding, and it is the
most important structural one after section 5.**

### Ambush is the preferred form

Scott states it flatly for the Visayas: "The preferred tactic on land was
ambush — *habon*, *saghid*, *hoom*, or *pool* — either by lying in wait or by
such strategies as exposing a few agile warriors to enemy view to lure them out
into the trap." He also records *sayang*, to pass by hidden enemies unawares,
and *laway*, a spy. The lexical density is itself the argument: a language with
four words for ambush and one for successfully sneaking past an ambush is
describing something people did constantly. **Documented, form uncertain** —
the vocabulary is drawn from seventeenth-century Visayan dictionaries
(Mentrida 1637, Alcina 1668), so the words are attested a generation or more
after the depicted period even though the practice plainly is not new.

The sixteenth-century eyewitness evidence agrees:

- **Mactan, 1521.** The islanders asked the Spanish to wait until morning "so
  that they might have more men. They said that in order to induce us to go in
  search of them; for they had dug certain pitholes between the houses in order
  that we might fall into them." Prepared ground, a delaying deception, and a
  trap. **Documented.**
- **Bombon, 1570.** A position "situated on both sides of the water, and thus
  very high and rugged, and suitable for laying ambuscades. This proved to be
  true; for suddenly, and without them being able to see any one, many arrows
  came flying through the air." **Documented.**
- **Static traps.** Scott records *balatik*, powerful set crossbows triggered by
  a cord across a path, "which could drive a shaft through a wild boar's body
  'like a gourd'", positioned along approaches to fortified refuges, together
  with *suyak*, ankle-high poisoned spikes planted in the ground. These are
  missile weapons that fight without a warrior present at all. **Documented,
  form uncertain** for their use against people as opposed to game.

### Raid, not battle

Scott's summary of purpose is unambiguous and matches what
[`ARMY-COMPOSITION.md`](../ARMY-COMPOSITION.md) already records: "Wars were
therefore fought to control people, not territory. They were waged by raids
intended to seize slaves outright, to initiate or enforce alliances for trading
networks, and to take booty to cover costs in any case. They were fought, not by
standing armies or navies loyal to some superordinate political authority, but
by citizen warriors owing personal allegiance to leaders who were physically
present."

The general term for war was *gubat*; engagements were *gahat* by land and
*mangayaw* by sea, with *salakay* for storming a fortified position and *bihag*
for taking by treachery. There is a word for storming a fort and a word for
treachery. **There is no word in Scott's list for a pitched field battle
between two arrayed forces.**

The deep-past track reached the same conclusion from archaeology
([Depth 3](../battles/03-deep-past-formations-and-tactics.md)): no evidence for
regular files or ranks, fixed frontage and depth, a shield wall, a spear block,
a bow or javelin screen, a formal reserve, encirclement doctrine, or alternating
missile and melee lines. Two entirely independent evidence bases — colonial
eyewitness testimony and excavation — both come up empty on pitched battle, and
that agreement is worth more than either on its own.

**This is not a problem for a battle simulation; it is a constraint on what the
simulation may claim.** Hukbo depicts two forces meeting on open ground because
that is the game it is, and that scenario is a legitimate abstraction. What it
may not do is present that scenario as the normal shape of sixteenth-century
Philippine warfare, because the sources say the normal shape was a raid, an
ambush, an evacuation, or a siege.

## 8. Ratio of missile troops to close fighters

**Answer: no source gives a ratio. The qualitative evidence points consistently
at "few", and at the bow being a regional rather than a general weapon.**

### What is actually stated

- **Morga, 1609, on the archipelago as a whole:** "The weapons of this people
  are, in some provinces, bow and arrows. But those generally used throughout
  the islands are moderate-sized spears with well-made points; and certain
  shields of light wood ... These cover them from top to toe, and are called
  *carasas* [*kalasag*]. At the waist they carry a dagger four fingers in
  breadth". The bow is explicitly the *provincial* case and spear-and-shield the
  general one. **Documented.**
- **Legazpi, c. 1565, on the Visayas:** "The weapons generally used throughout
  the Filipinas are cutlasses and daggers; lances with iron points ...
  *lenguados*, enclosed in cloth sheaths, and a few bows and arrows."
  **Documented**, and note "a few".
- **The 1569 relation:** bows "very strong and large, and much more powerful
  than those used by the English", with unfeathered reed arrows and hardwood
  points — but the weapon list opens with shields, lances, and cutlasses, and
  the bow appears after them. **Documented.**
- **Scott's synthesis, Visayas:** "the bow and arrow had scattered distribution,
  and the blowgun was found only in Mindanao and Palawan ... The small role
  assigned the bow and arrow in Visayan warfare is suggested by the poverty of
  its vocabulary ... Archers were reported in most places but in small numbers,
  and their lack of skill was regularly contrasted with the expertise of
  mountain Negritos, or Sangil islanders". **Documented** as Scott's reading of
  the whole corpus.
- **Scott on the common missile, which is not the bow:** "Normal for open combat
  were bamboo spears with fire-hardened wooden points, their last section loaded
  with sand for better balance, or, most common of all, the *sugob*, a length of
  sharpened *bagakay* bamboo." **Documented, form uncertain.**

### The one hard number, and what it counts

Rada's 1577 report of "a chieftain of the Zambals ... with 100 archers" is the
only headcount of missile troops anywhere in this pass. It counts an **allied
contingent hired for a Spanish campaign against a Chinese pirate**, recruited
from a people specifically noted for archery and head-taking. It is not a
proportion of a Philippine war party, and it cannot be turned into one.

### The claim that had to be rejected

Enthusiast writing states that 1,500 Filipino archers accompanied Sande's 1578
Borneo expedition. This pass looked for that figure in the Sande papers in
[Volume IV](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/12635) and did not find it. What is
there is a different campaign and a different number: the 1575 Pangasinan
expedition against Limahong, "about two hundred and fifty-six [Spaniards],
together with two thousand five hundred friendly Indians ... in fifty-nine
native vessels", with **no breakdown by weapon at all**. The 1,500-archer figure
is recorded here as **not verified in this pass** and must not be used.

### Verdict for the game

A roster in which missile-armed warriors are a **clear minority**, are
**regionally rather than uniformly distributed**, and in which the most common
thrown weapon is a **cheap bamboo javelin rather than a bow**, is the reading
best supported by the evidence. Any specific percentage is a gameplay tuning
value with no evidentiary confidence at all, in exactly the sense
[`HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md`](../HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md) already uses for
the combat preset's target weights.
## 9. Naval versus land: how much of the evidence is boat fighting?

**Answer: most of it, and the transfer to a land battle is weak enough that it
should be treated as a separate question rather than an assumption.**

### The audit

Counting the engagements in which Philippine missile fire is actually described:

| Engagement | Setting | Missile evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mactan 1521 | Amphibious — Spanish in thigh- and knee-deep water, islanders on the beach | The richest missile description in the corpus |
| Bombon 1570, river approach | Riverine — *prau* under fire from both banks | Arrows from concealment |
| Bombon 1570, the plain | **Land** | Arrows "as rapidly as they could", then flight |
| Manila 1570 | Riverbank town, boats in the river | Culverins, one arrow wound; town stormed |
| Lubang 1570 | **Land**, siege of two forts and a rock | Culverins and "a few arrows" across a moat, three hours |
| Bangkusay 1571 | Naval | Culverins and "many arrows" from twenty to thirty boats |
| Cainta 1572 | **Land**, assault on a palisaded town | One small culverin, one palm-wood lance |
| Sarangani 1543 | **Land**, contested fields | Bowmen "good marksmen in the extreme", fifty Spanish dead over three months |
| Rio Grande de Mindanao 1579 | Riverine — archers arrive and leave by *baroto* | Repeated arrow exchanges across a river |
| Tapaca 1579 | Riverine | Culverins and arrows at a fleet |
| Karakoa fighting decks | Naval | The only description of massed missile throwing anywhere |

Roughly half the engagements are riverine or naval outright, and of the
"land" cases, two are sieges of fortifications and one is a three-month
agricultural harassment. **The number of engagements in this corpus that are
open-field, land, force-on-force missile actions is one — Bombon 1570 — and it
lasted a single exchange before the Filipino side ran.**

### Why the transfer is weak

A boat imposes conditions a field does not:

1. **The platform is fixed and elevated.** Morga's bamboo gangway above the
   rowers puts a row of fighting men shoulder to shoulder, facing outward, with
   nowhere to go. Concentration of missile fire is a *consequence of the hull*,
   not a tactical choice that survives being put ashore.
2. **The fighters and the rowers are the same people, and there are far more
   rowers.** Morga's largest type carried "one hundred rowers on a side and
   thirty soldiers above to fight". A missile-per-man ratio derived from a
   fighting deck is a ratio of the platform, not of the society.
   [`ARMY-COMPOSITION.md`](../ARMY-COMPOSITION.md) §6 already forbids splitting
   sailors from warriors as permanent classes, and the same warning applies
   here in reverse: do not read the thirty men on the platform as a missile arm.
3. **Ranges are set by the water.** Ships close, or they do not; the crew cannot
   choose to advance thirty paces. The documented naval fights are decided by
   whether one fleet accepts contact, which is the same question as section 6
   but with the obstacle built into the vehicle.
4. **Ammunition on a boat is stowed.** The Mactan behavior of picking up and
   re-throwing the same spear four or six times is a beach behavior. A karakoa
   carries its javelins aboard.

### Consequence

**Every claim in this document that rests on karakoa fighting decks, on
"pouring volleys into enemy ships at close quarters", or on arrows fired across
a river is naval or riverine evidence, and it is marked as such wherever it is
used.** A land-battle simulation that imports the concentrated-fire picture from
the fighting deck is importing a boat's geometry onto a field. That is a
**game-design invention**, and it must be labelled as one.

## 10. The arquebus specifically

**Answer: documented, rare, imported, boat-carried, and judged by the man who
sent one to Spain to be carried "more to terrify than to kill". Its arrival did
not change Philippine tactics in this corpus; it changed the outcome of
Philippine fights against Spaniards.**

### Who had them

Legazpi, writing from Cebu on 15 July 1567 and sending weapon specimens to the
crown ([Volume II](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/13280)):

> It seemed to me that your highness would be pleased with specimens of the
> weapons with which these natives fight; accordingly they are bringing to your
> highness a Chinese arquebuse, of which there are some among these natives.
> Although they are very dexterous in handling these guns, when on the sea,
> aboard of their *praus*, they carry them more to terrify than to kill.

Four things in one sentence, all **Documented**:

1. The guns were **Chinese imports**, not local manufacture.
2. There were **some** — Legazpi's word, and he chose it while trying to
   impress a king with the strangeness of the place.
3. They were carried **aboard *praus***, at sea.
4. Legazpi's own reading of their purpose is **psychological**: to terrify
   rather than to kill. That is an observer's judgement, not a measurement, and
   should be labelled as his opinion — but it is the opinion of the best-placed
   observer in the corpus.

Morga confirms the direction of travel forty years later: "Since they have seen
the Spaniards use their weapons, many of the natives handle the arquebuses and
muskets quite skilfully. Before the arrival of the Spaniards they had bronze
culverins and other pieces of cast iron, with which they defended their forts
and settlements." **The pre-contact firearm of the archipelago is the swivel gun
on a wall or a boat, not the shoulder arm.** Scott agrees: "firearms were known
only to Moros", and the Visayan word for an arquebus, *bitbang*, is a Malay
loanword.

### How they were used, and what changed

The corpus gives no example of Filipinos using arquebuses in a field action.
What it gives instead is:

- **Emplaced artillery in forts and boats,** everywhere: thirteen pieces taken
  at Manila in 1570, ten or twelve culverins per side of each Lubang fort, one
  or two per boat at Bangkusay, five or six in the Bangkusay shore fort, four at
  Cainta, and casting moulds in Sulayman's store-room for a piece seventeen feet
  long. **Documented.**
- **A documented judgement that the local gunnery was bad.** Scott's reading of
  Bangkusay is that the Filipino fleet "failed to inflict any damage on the
  Spanish ships — no doubt because Southeast Asian gunnery at the time was
  simply a matter of pointing guns at the enemy without taking aim". That is
  Scott's inference, **Provisional reconstruction**, but it is consistent with a
  Brunei report in Sande's papers that its people "do not fear the arquebuses
  very much, because they themselves are so poor shots with them, and are amazed
  at seeing a hen or a pigeon killed with an arquebuse-shot. They fear lances
  more than other weapons." **Documented** as a report about Brunei.
- **A documented counter-tactic.** At Manila in 1570 the Spanish learned from
  friendly rowers that the Moros "were planning to fall upon the Spaniards at
  the first rain, when it would be impossible for them to make use of the
  arquebuses". The rain came only after the town had burned. **Documented**, and
  it is the single most sophisticated piece of tactical reasoning attributed to
  a Philippine commander anywhere in this corpus: identify the matchlock's
  dependence on dry powder and match, and schedule the attack around the
  weather.

### What changed when firearms appeared

Not Philippine tactics. What changed was that **archery stopped working against
the people who had firearms**, and it changed fast:

- Sarangani 1543: the archers "could penetrate Spanish breastplates" and were
  "good marksmen", "But Spanish firearms ... soon turned the tide of battle, and
  the defenders were driven back into the hills."
- Bombon 1570: they shot until arquebus balls hit their flanks, then ran.
- Bangkusay 1571: they shot until the arquebusiers were in range, then broke.

**Documented.** The consistent picture is that missile troops who could not be
reached did well, and missile troops who could be reached by a superior missile
weapon left. That is a statement about an asymmetric colonial encounter, and it
must not be recycled as a statement about how Filipinos fought each other.

## 11. Where did a missile-armed man stand relative to the shield-and-blade line?

**Answer: the question presumes a line, and no source describes one. What the
sources describe is a missile-armed man standing behind a wall, on a hill, on a
boat, in cover, or across a river — never in a stated relationship to a body of
close fighters.**

### Positions that are documented

1. **On or behind a fortification.** "They are accustomed to fortify themselves
   in strong positions, where they mount their artillery and archery" (1569
   relation). Bikol *bantayan* archer towers. Lubang's walls. **Documented.**
2. **On the elevated fighting deck of a boat, above the rowers.** Morga.
   **Documented.**
3. **Hidden, in broken or elevated ground beside a route.** Bombon 1570;
   Sarangani 1543; the Mindanao river bank 1579. **Documented.**
4. **In the same crowd as everyone else.** At Mactan, arrows, bamboo spears,
   fire-hardened stakes, stones, and mud come at the Spanish simultaneously from
   the same three divisions that then close and kill Magellan. Pigafetta gives
   no hint of separate missile and close bodies. **Documented, form uncertain** —
   the absence of a distinction in his account is not proof there was none.

### The individual carried both, which dissolves the question

The strongest structural finding in this section is that the sixteenth-century
Philippine fighter was not specialised the way a European or a wargame roster
is. The evidence is repeated and consistent:

- **Cagayan Sulu, 1521:** "They have blowpipes and small quivers at their side,
  full of arrows and a poisonous herb. They have daggers whose hafts are
  adorned with gold and precious gems, spears, bucklers, and small cuirasses of
  buffalo horn." One man, a blowgun, a dagger, a spear, and a shield.
  **Documented.**
- **Legazpi's specimen shipment, 1567:** lances, daggers, a cutlass, two
  corselets, two helmets, "and a bow with quiver and arrows, all which they
  use" — a single undifferentiated kit for "the weapons with which these natives
  fight". **Documented.**
- **Morga, 1609:** every man carries the *bararao* dagger at the waist,
  regardless of what else he has. **Documented.**
- **Scott:** "Bladed weapons were an ordinary part of Visayan male costume, at
  least a dagger or spear." **Documented.**

A warrior who throws a bamboo javelin and then fights with the spear and shield
he already carries is not a "ranged unit" who needs a position relative to a
"melee line". He is a fighter whose behavior changes with distance. **That is
the historically defensible model, and it is a much better fit for an emergent
agent simulation than a role-based line anyway.**

The dedicated specialist does exist in the record, but as a **separate people**:
the Negrito archers, the Sangil bowmen sent to Mindanao allies, the hundred
Zambal archers who came for Chinese heads. Where a force had a genuine archer
element, it was usually somebody else's contingent, with its own chief and its
own price. **Documented.**

## 12. What happened when a missile-armed man was reached?

**Answer: he had a sidearm, and in at least two documented cases the missile
weapon itself was the sidearm. But the commonest documented outcome is that
nobody waited to find out — the shooters left, or were killed because they could
not.**

### He carried a blade, and the sources are unanimous

Section 11's evidence is the answer to this question as well. Every weapon list
in the corpus pairs missiles with a dagger, a cutlass, a spear, or all three.
Morga has the *bararao* at every waist. Scott has at least a dagger or spear as
ordinary male costume. There is no attested figure in this record who carries
only a missile weapon.

### The blowgun converts into a spear

The single most concrete answer in the whole pass comes from Pigafetta on
Palawan in 1521:

> At the end of their blowpipes they fasten a bit of iron like a spear head;
> and when they have shot all their arrows they fight with that.

Scott confirms it independently for Sarangani and Palawan: the *sumpit* "was
also fitted with an iron spearhead for use after all ammunition had been spent."
**Documented.** A weapon designed so that running out of ammunition converts it
into a short spear is a design statement about what its user expected to happen
next.

### Withdrawal is the documented behavior, and it is not a rout

Three separate patterns, all **Documented**:

1. **Break and run when contact becomes unavoidable.** Bombon 1570 and Bangkusay
   1571, both the moment arquebus fire reached them. At Lubang, "when they saw
   that half of our people were already on the wall, and the rest in the act of
   mounting, they all turned their backs and fled" — and the fort had been built
   with "a passage-way which they had made for that very purpose". A prepared
   line of retreat is not panic; it is design.
2. **Fight back at contact and die.** The fourteen Mindanao archers in 1579
   refused to stop shooting under arquebus fire, and when six soldiers and some
   allied Filipinos crossed the river, "the Indians who had fired the said arrows
   began to defend themselves. Therefore the friendly Indians killed three of
   them and took one prisoner." They had blades and used them; four of fourteen
   were caught; the rest got away.
3. **Turn and strike the pursuer.** Scott records the Visayan word
   *pinaorihiyan*, "for a fleeing warrior to turn and spear his pursuer", and
   *naga kamatayan*, to fight to the death, and *mangin matay*, a man determined
   to die on the field. **Documented, form uncertain** — the vocabulary is
   seventeenth-century, but a language does not coin a single word for
   "turn and spear your pursuer" unless people did it.

### What is not supported

- **Dropping the weapon.** Nothing in this corpus describes a warrior
  abandoning a bow, blowgun, or spear to fight. Given that these were valuable
  personal property, that expensive spears were thrown only when retrievable,
  and that a blowgun was built with a spearhead on the end, the reconstruction
  runs the other way: **the weapon was kept**. **Provisional reconstruction.**
- **A protected withdrawal through friendly close fighters.** Not attested. See
  section 4.
- **Any rule about surrender.** Loarca records that killing a captive after his
  surrender cost the killer his own price in gold, and that a captured chief was
  ransomed at double — so capture was economically preferred to killing — but
  nothing describes the moment of surrender itself in a fight. **Documented**
  for the economics, **not supported** for the mechanic.

## 13. Comparative Southeast Asian evidence, and how far it may be carried

Michael W. Charney's *Southeast Asian Warfare, 1300-1900* (Brill, 2004) is the
standard comparative treatment and was named as a key text for this pass. It
must be reported honestly: **the book itself was not opened in this pass.** It
is not freely available in full text, and this document will not paraphrase a
book it has not read. What follows is therefore limited to what reviews and
publisher material establish about its structure and its arguments, and is
marked accordingly.

**What is established about the book, from secondary description:**

- It is organised thematically rather than as a narrative, covering the cultural
  features of regional warfare, the main weapons, and the two principal sites of
  fighting — **sieges and naval contests** — with a dedicated chapter on
  firearms. That the book's two structural categories of "where fighting
  happened" are sieges and naval actions rather than field battles is itself
  consistent with the picture in sections 6 and 7 above.
- Reviewers note that the book engages the historiographical position that
  "flight" rather than "fight", and defence rather than offence, dominated
  Southeast Asian warfare, and that Charney argues against reading the region's
  warfare as either bloodless ritual or as lacking modern character.
- Reviewers also warn that Charney sometimes generalises across the region from
  a single country's evidence. That warning matters here more than usual,
  because the whole point of citing him would be to fill a Philippine gap with
  mainland or Malay evidence — which is exactly the operation the historical
  accuracy policy in [`CLAUDE.md`](../../../CLAUDE.md) section 7 forbids when it
  says not to generalise one region to another.

**Rule for any future use.** Comparative Southeast Asian evidence may be used to
show that a Philippine reading is *not implausible*. It may never be used to
raise a Philippine claim's confidence tier. If a behavior is **Provisional
reconstruction** on Philippine evidence, a Burmese or Javanese parallel leaves
it a **Provisional reconstruction**. This is the same rule that keeps
seventeenth-century Visayan vocabulary from silently upgrading a
sixteenth-century tactical claim.

Reading Charney properly is recorded as open question 3 in section 17.

## 14. Community and enthusiast claims, recorded as community claims

Community and enthusiast writing is where most people actually meet this
subject, so it is worth recording what it says and where it goes wrong. **None
of the claims in this section may be promoted to Documented on the strength of
the sources cited here.** They are recorded as **community claims** — a label
outside the three-tier system entirely, because they are not evidence.

### The English Wikipedia article

[Warfare in pre-colonial Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warfare_in_pre-colonial_Philippines)
carries maintenance banners for insufficient citation and possible original
research, dated December 2019, and its most-reused citation is a tourism
website. On tactics it offers raiding, scorched earth attributed to Sri Lumay
of Cebu, ambush, and artillery in forts and boats. It makes **no claim at all**
about a missile phase, skirmishers, volley fire, standoff distance, ranged-to-
melee ratios, or what happened to archers in melee — which is worth recording,
because it means the popular account is silent on precisely the questions this
document was asked.

**Its most consequential error is the one identified in section 4.** The article
cites Sande's Manila report for a description of "men carrying battle-axes in
their squadron, and some well-placed arquebusiers" as the earliest description
of *native* fighting methods. The passage describes **Limahong's Chinese landing
force**. The Zambals appear a few paragraphs earlier and are described only as
bow-users and head-takers. Any Philippine "skirmisher" claim traced back to this
passage is traceable to a misread source.

### Filipino martial arts and enthusiast sites

The FMA-adjacent article
[Feared Warriors: The Archers of the Philippines](https://www.fightingsticks.de/en/blog/feared-warriors-the-archers-of-the-philippines)
claims that war bows were taller than the shooter and braced with the foot,
that Zambales arrowheads were drilled to shatter inside the body, and that 1,500
Filipino archers joined Sande's 1578 Borneo expedition. Its only cited sources
are Gemelli Careri, a **late-seventeenth-century Italian traveller**, and an
1899 American book. The 1,500-archer figure was checked against the Sande papers
in this pass and not found; see section 8. The long-bow-braced-with-the-foot
detail does have an independent echo in Scott's Bikol *sikarom* entry, but that
rests on Lisboa's 1628 dictionary, so the correct tier for it is **Documented,
form uncertain**, and it applies to Bikol, not to the archipelago.

Sites such as `sinaunangpanahon.com` and the Fandom military wiki repeat a
"guerrilla warfare" framing that is broadly compatible with the ambush evidence
in section 7, but they do so without citation and with modern military
vocabulary that the sources do not license.

### AI-generated reference pages

Searches for this topic now surface AI-written reference pages that read as
authoritative and cite nothing checkable. One such page supplied a confident
account of *mangayaw* raiding, dawn attacks, and withdrawal after taking
captives. Every element of that account happens to be defensible from Scott —
which is exactly what makes the genre dangerous, because an equally confident
page with an invented detail would be indistinguishable. **No AI-generated
reference page is cited in this document, and none should be cited in this
repository.**

### What the community gets right

Two things, and they should be acknowledged:

1. **Raiding, not pitched battle.** The community consensus that pre-colonial
   Philippine warfare was raid-centred is correct and matches Scott and the
   primary corpus. Section 7.
2. **Ambush as the preferred tactic.** Also correct, also matches Scott.

The community is right about the strategic shape and unreliable about the
tactical detail — which is the expected pattern, since the strategic shape is
what the sources actually record.

## 15. What a 500-agent tactical simulation can and cannot honestly claim

This section exists because the evidence above does not add up to a battle
simulation, and pretending otherwise would be the failure mode the historical
accuracy policy exists to prevent. It uses the three categories already
established in
[`FORMATION_AND_COLLISION_MECHANICS.md`](../FORMATION_AND_COLLISION_MECHANICS.md):
**historical evidence**, **tactical simulation inference**, and **game-design
invention**.

### What the game can claim as historical evidence

These are supportable at **Documented** or **Documented, form uncertain**, and a
spectator-facing inspector note could cite a source for each:

- Missile weapons are used before contact and continue to be used during it.
- Shooting is individual and aimed at whoever is in front of the shooter, at a
  rate set by the individual.
- A shooter picks a target's exposed part when he can see one — the Mactan
  legs.
- Thrown weapons are retrieved and re-thrown when the thrower can reach them.
- A missile-armed warrior carries a blade and uses it when reached.
- A blowgun becomes a short spear when its darts are gone.
- Bows are a minority weapon, unevenly distributed, and a bamboo javelin is the
  common missile.
- Forces are coalitions of separately led followings, and a leader's people
  advance, hesitate, and withdraw with him and not with the army.
- Withdrawal is a normal, non-shameful outcome, including withdrawal by the
  whole community.
- Locally held firearms are rare, imported, and associated with boats and
  fortifications rather than with a field line.

### What the game must present as tactical simulation inference

Defensible as consequences of geometry and bodies, but not recovered history:

- Missile-armed agents need space and line of sight, and crowding degrades
  both.
- A shooter with an enemy inside blade reach is in a different situation from a
  shooter with clear ground, and behaves differently.
- Concentration of fire emerges when many individuals in one place have the same
  target available, without anyone commanding it.
- Distance, cover, and elevation change who can be hit.

### What the game must label as game-design invention

Every one of the following would have to be **invented**, and the sources
support none of them. If any of them ships, it ships as an explicitly labelled
game mechanic, in the same style as the provisional shield multiplier already in
the code:

1. **Any volley, salvo, or fire-discipline mechanic.** Section 5. There is no
   command apparatus in the record for one to run on.
2. **A skirmisher screen, a screen-withdrawal behavior, or an
   interpenetration rule.** Section 4. Not attested, and the passage usually
   cited for it is about Chinese pirates.
3. **A fixed ranged-to-melee ratio in a roster.** Section 8. "A minority" is
   supportable; a number is not.
4. **Any archer "stand behind the line" positioning rule.** Section 11. There is
   no line.
5. **Ammunition counts, quiver sizes, reload times, or a resupply rule.** No
   source gives any of these. Note also that
   [`CLAUDE.md`](../../../CLAUDE.md) section 9 forbids projectile ammunition
   before the gate that authorises it, so this is a constraint from two
   directions at once.
6. **Effective ranges in metres or game units.** No source gives a range for any
   Philippine missile weapon. The 1569 relation says blowgun darts do not carry
   as far as arrows, and that is the entire evidentiary content on range in the
   corpus.
7. **Hit probability, damage, or armour penetration values.** Sarangani's
   arrows "could penetrate Spanish breastplates" and Mactan's musket balls
   "only passed through the shields which were made of thin wood" are anecdotes
   about specific moments, not measurements.
8. **Poison as a mechanic.** Poisoned arrows and darts are well attested, but
   [`HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md`](../HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md) already rules
   that they "must not be presented as universal", and no source supports a
   universal effect, a dose, or a time to death beyond Loarca's and Scott's
   folk descriptions.
9. **A morale or suppression model.** The break-and-run behavior at Bombon and
   Bangkusay is documented as an *outcome*, and a mechanic that produces it is a
   hypothesis about a cause. Morale is also on the forbidden list in
   [`CLAUDE.md`](../../../CLAUDE.md) section 9.
10. **Any firearm "terror" or fear effect.** Legazpi's "more to terrify than to
    kill" is a real quotation and a genuinely attractive mechanic, and it is one
    man's opinion about why other men carried a gun on a boat. Implementing it
    would be an invention wearing a citation.

### The three honest sentences

If a design document needs to state the position in three sentences, these are
them:

1. **The sources support a fight in which individuals shoot at whoever they can
   see, from wherever they happen to be, until someone closes or someone
   leaves.**
2. **The sources do not support a missile phase with structure — no volleys, no
   screens, no lines, no ratios, no ranges — and every structure the game adds
   is a gameplay model chosen for readability and determinism, not a historical
   claim.**
3. **The scenario the game depicts, two comparable forces meeting on open
   ground and fighting to a decision, is itself the largest invention in the
   product, because the record says warfare here was raid, ambush, siege, and
   evacuation.**

That third sentence is not an argument against building the game. It is the
sentence that has to appear somewhere a player can read it.

### The spectator test

[`SIMULATION-GAME-STANDARDS.md`](../../../SIMULATION-GAME-STANDARDS.md) §10 asks
whether a spectator can discover an effect without reading source code. For
ranged behavior the answers are unusually clean, and they sort the feature list
better than the evidence does:

- **A warrior throwing a javelin, then drawing a blade when reached, is visible.**
  Build it.
- **A blowgunner reversing his tube into a spear is visible.** Build it, and put
  the Pigafetta line in the inspector.
- **A shooter aiming at an unarmoured part is visible only through the existing
  hit-location inspector**, which already exists and already carries the correct
  "gameplay model" framing.
- **A ratio, a range value, or a reload time is not visible at all.** A
  spectator cannot discover any of them, which means they fail the standard's
  test on their own terms before the historical question is even reached.
## 16. What the sources do not establish

Listed explicitly, because the gap is where invented content appears. Each of
these was searched for in this pass and not found.

**About missile fighting specifically:**

- Any volley, salvo, or simultaneous discharge on a command.
- Any signal — voice, horn, gong, drum, or flag — that started or stopped
  shooting. (Morga's sung rowing refrains time an oar stroke, not a shot.)
- Any missile screen forward of a main body, and any withdrawal of one.
- Any stated effective range for a bow, a blowgun, a javelin, or a culverin.
- Any ammunition quantity, quiver capacity, or resupply arrangement.
- Any statement that a force ran out of ammunition, except the implication in
  the Palawan blowgun's spearhead.
- Any ratio, proportion, or headcount of missile troops within a Philippine war
  party.
- Any rate of fire.
- Any account of Filipinos using shoulder-arm firearms in a land action.
- Any description of how a missile-armed man was protected, relieved, or
  replaced.
- Any wound or casualty statistic attributable to missiles as against blades.

**About the shape of a fight:**

- Any Philippine account of a Philippine battle. Every eyewitness is Spanish.
- Any description of two Philippine forces fighting each other on land, in
  detail, by an observer.
- Any second attestation of Mactan's three divisions. It remains the only
  formation described in the corpus, exactly as
  [`ARMY-COMPOSITION.md`](../ARMY-COMPOSITION.md) §5 records.
- Any named formation, drill, or training regime.
- Any rule for when a fight ended, or a surrender or truce procedure inside a
  battle.
- Any distinction, anywhere, between a missile specialist and a close fighter
  within the same following. The specialists in the record are separate
  peoples supplying allied contingents.

**About things frequently assumed:**

- That the 1582 Cagayan battles are evidence about Philippine tactics. They are
  a Spanish action against Japanese *wokou* raiders, the popular "tercio versus
  samurai" framing is contested on its own terms, and no Philippine missile
  behavior is described in them. They are recorded here only to close the
  question.
- That the Boxer Codex text describes tactics. Its images are used elsewhere in
  this repository for silhouette and costume; its accompanying text was **not
  opened in this pass**, and it remains open question 2 below.

Absence from this list is an evidence limit, not proof that something did not
happen. It is, however, a hard boundary on what Hukbo may assert.

---

(the battlefield realism design) fills a contingent from

## 17. Open questions for a later research pass

1. **The Spanish originals behind the word "volley".** Section 5 turns on the
   claim that Blair and Robertson's "volley" translates an idiom for a shower of
   arrows rather than a commanded discharge. That claim is a reasonable reading
   of an early-twentieth-century translation convention, but it was **not
   verified against the Spanish text** in this pass. Checking the four passages
   — Bombon 1570, Tapaca 1579, and the two Mindanao 1579 passages — against the
   Spanish would either confirm the strongest finding in this document or
   overturn it. **This is the highest-value follow-up in the list.**
2. **The Boxer Codex text.** It falls inside the target window, it describes
   peoples by their fighting reputation, and no one in this repository has read
   it. [`ARMY-COMPOSITION.md`](../ARMY-COMPOSITION.md) §12 already carries the
   same open item.
3. **Charney, *Southeast Asian Warfare, 1300-1900*.** Not opened in this pass;
   see section 13. Its chapters on naval contests and firearms are the two most
   relevant to this document, and its comparative material would clarify whether
   the Philippine picture is regionally typical or unusual.
4. **Alcina (1668) and Mentrida (1637) at first hand.** Scott's tactical
   vocabulary — the four words for ambush, *pinaorihiyan*, *asdang*, *bangga* —
   comes from these seventeenth-century dictionaries. Reading them directly
   would let each term carry its own attestation date instead of inheriting
   Scott's compression, which matters because the hundred-year rule applies term
   by term.
5. **Whether the "shoot at the legs" behavior recurs.** Mactan's aimed fire at
   unarmoured legs is the single strongest piece of evidence for individual
   aiming. If a second independent attestation of deliberate aiming at an
   exposed part exists anywhere in the corpus, section 5's verdict moves from
   strong to unassailable.
6. **Junker's Tanjay skeletal evidence read for wound type.** The deep-past
   track records trauma and disputed mass burial at Tanjay
   ([Depth 1](../battles/01-deep-past-overall-warfare.md)). Whether any of that
   trauma is projectile trauma would be the only physical evidence about missile
   use in the entire repository, and it has not been checked.
7. **Mindanao and Sulu.** As with rank and army composition, the missile
   evidence for the south is thin and mostly comes from Spanish river
   expeditions. A Mindanao faction would need its own pass against sources of
   the right date.
