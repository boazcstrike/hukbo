# Ranged weapons of the pre-colonial and early-contact Philippines, c. 1500s

Research compiled 2026-08-07. Companion to
[HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md](../HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md) and
[WEAPON_CLASH_1500s.md](../WEAPON_CLASH_1500s.md), and bound by the same
evidence-labelling rules and by the historical accuracy policy in
`CLAUDE.md` section 7.

**Status: research only. This document does not authorize implementation, and
it does not define gameplay statistics.** Where it gives a number for range or
rate of fire, that number is either sourced or explicitly marked as reasoning
from physical form; in neither case is it a tuning authorization. A design
document and a plan document have to come first, exactly as section 6 of
`CLAUDE.md` requires.

## Scope and inherited rules

This document covers four ranged weapons and no others:

1. **Bangkaw — Long Spear**, in its thrown role
2. **Busog — War Bow**
3. **Sumpit — Blowgun**
4. **Imported Arquebus**

The rules inherited from `HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` bind every claim below,
and where anything found in a web search conflicts with that document, that
document wins.

- **The research boundary.** "The Philippines in the 1500s" was not one
  military culture. The usable evidence clusters around Mactan and Cebu in
  1521, Visayan and Luzon encounters between 1565 and 1582, the Manila-area
  campaigns around 1570, and the late-century visual material of the Boxer
  Codex compiled in Manila around 1590 to 1595. Nothing here may be
  generalized to the whole archipelago or the whole century.
- **The three confidence labels.** *Documented* means directly described or
  pictured in a sixteenth-century source. *Documented, form uncertain* means
  the weapon class is attested but its exact local form or name is uncertain.
  *Provisional reconstruction* means a plausible identification supported by
  later traditions or objects but not firmly established for the cited event.
- **The hundred-year name rule.** A name whose earliest attestation postdates
  the depicted period by more than a century is not used at all. This is the
  rule that excluded the panabas. Every name proposed below is checked against
  it explicitly.
- **Pair form only.** A cultural identification appears in player-facing UI
  only as the Filipino name, an em dash, and a plain English descriptor, and
  never as a bare label.
- **Spanish accounts are evidence about equipment, not neutral ethnography.**
  The Boxer Codex guides silhouette and colour, not exact technical
  cataloguing.

## 1. Bangkaw — Long Spear (thrown)

**Confidence label: Documented.**

The thrown spear is the best-attested ranged weapon in the entire
sixteenth-century Philippine record. It is documented not merely as present but
in action, at close quarters, with its projectiles named, its targets named,
and its resupply behaviour described. Nothing else on this list is attested
that thoroughly.

### Earliest attestation

Antonio Pigafetta, present at Mactan on 27 April 1521, describes the throwing
directly. In the Robertson translation printed in Blair and Robertson,
*The Philippine Islands, 1493–1898*, volume XXXIII:

> They shot so many arrows at us and hurled so many bamboo spears (some of them
> tipped with iron) at the captain-general, besides pointed stakes hardened with
> fire, stones, and mud, that we could scarcely defend ourselves.

The same account gives the pre-battle exchange, in which the Mactan side names
its own weapons:

> They replied that if we had lances they had lances of bamboo and stakes
> hardened with fire.

And it gives the single most mechanically useful line in the whole corpus for a
thrown-weapon system:

> The natives continued to pursue us, and picking up the same spear four or six
> times, hurled it at us again and again.

Two further sentences establish that the thrown spear, not the bow, did the
decisive close work: an attacker "hurled a bamboo spear into the captain's
face", and Magellan could not draw his sword "because he had been wounded in
the arm with a bamboo spear". Text from
[Project Gutenberg ebook 42884, Blair and Robertson volume XXXIII](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/42884).

Two independent later sources confirm the class outside Mactan and outside the
Visayas. García Descalante Alvarado, who sailed with Villalobos and wrote his
account at Lisbon on 7 August 1548 about events of roughly 1543 to 1545 around
Sarangani and Mindanao, lists among the offensive arms "lances, javelins, and
other missile weapons", and separately describes a defence mounted with "small
stones, poles, arrows, and mangrove cudgels as large around as the arm, the
ends sharpened and hardened in the fire". Text in
[Blair and Robertson volume II](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/13280).

The anonymous *Relation of the Conquest of the Island of Luzon* (Manila,
20 April 1572, describing the campaign of 1570) adds the material distinction
that matters most for a thrown weapon:

> The small weapons used by these natives are badly tempered iron lances, which
> become blunt upon striking a fairly good coat of mail, a kind of broad dagger,
> and arrows — which are weapons of little value. Other lances are also used
> which are made of fire-hardened palm-wood and are harder than the iron ones.

Text in [Blair and Robertson volume III](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/13616).

### Name check

**The name passes, and it passes on a 1521 attestation.** Pigafetta's Visayan
vocabulary, collected in 1521 and printed in Blair and Robertson volume XXXIII,
contains the entry:

> for Spear — bancan

Blair and Robertson's editors set Pigafetta's phonetic renderings against the
Visayan dictionaries of Juan Félix de la Encarnación (1885) and Antonio Sanchez
de la Rosa (1895) in a comparative table, and identify this word directly:

| English | Pigafetta | Encarnación | Sanchez |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| spear | bancan | bangcao | bangcao |

The editors mark conjectural equivalences in that table with a question mark.
This row is not marked, so the identification is theirs as a positive claim
rather than a guess. That said, the honest description of the evidence is that
Pigafetta wrote *bancan* and the final *-w* is supplied by the sixteenth-century
word's identification with the modern Visayan *bangkaw* rather than being
written down in 1521. The gap between attestation and the depicted period is
therefore zero years, and the hundred-year rule is cleared with room to spare
even if a reader discounts the phonetic bridge entirely.

Two cautions on the name. First, *bangkaw* is a Visayan, Mindanao, and Maranao
word, not a Tagalog one; the Tagalog term is *sibat*
([Wiktionary, bangkaw](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/bangkaw)). Using
*bangkaw* across the whole archipelago would be exactly the regional
generalisation `CLAUDE.md` section 7 forbids, so the label has to be understood
as Visayan-anchored. Second, modern Filipino martial-arts usage is inconsistent
about whether *bangkaw* means a spear or a plain staff, with practitioners
splitting both ways. That inconsistency is community usage and carries no
weight against a 1521 vocabulary entry, but it is a reason not to lean on
modern FMA glosses when writing the tooltip.

### Player-facing pair label

**Bangkaw — Long Spear**, which is the label
`HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` already recommends. No change is needed. If the
thrown role has to be distinguished in the interface from a thrusting role, the
honest way to do it is a range-category chip reading `thrown` rather than a
second invented Filipino name.

### Physical form

The sources describe at least two distinct objects and the game should not
merge them.

The **thrown object at Mactan** is a bamboo spear, some examples tipped with
iron and some not, thrown alongside fire-hardened wooden stakes and stones.
Pigafetta's wording implies a light, cheap, expendable shaft — which is exactly
what makes the four-to-six-times reuse plausible. No length is given.

The **iron-headed lance** described by Diego de Artieda in his *Relation of the
Western Islands Called Filipinas* (1573) is a different and much heavier
object:

> lances, two and a half *varas* long, with iron and steel points a third as
> long as the lance, and as wide as the hand.

A Castilian *vara* is approximately 0.836 m, so this is a shaft of roughly
2.1 m carrying a head of roughly 0.7 m — a very large blade-head lance, and
the Artieda passage explicitly notes that "in some districts the lance-points
are long and ground to a very fine edge". Nothing in Artieda says this weapon
was thrown, and a 2.1 m spear with a 0.7 m head is not a javelin. The
*Relation of the Conquest of the Island of Luzon* adds a third variant, the
fire-hardened palm-wood lance, and states it was harder than the iron-headed
one.

Ammunition carried is not stated anywhere. The only evidence bearing on it is
the reuse line at Mactan, which describes an army fighting on its own ground
over a shoreline littered with spent shafts — a resupply situation that does
not generalise to an open-field engagement.

### Range and rate of fire

**No source gives a throwing distance.** Every number below is reasoning from
the physical form and from cross-cultural comparison, and is labelled as such.

- **Effective range: roughly 10 to 20 m; outer limit roughly 25 to 35 m for a
  light shaft thrown for distance rather than for effect.** This is the
  standard band for a hand-thrown military spear across cultures and is not a
  Philippine measurement. Pigafetta's account is consistent with it only in the
  weak sense that the throwing happened at a range where the throwers were
  simultaneously exposed to sword and lance work.
- **Rate of fire: roughly one throw every 3 to 5 seconds while shafts are to
  hand**, from the mechanics of picking up, setting, and throwing a light
  shaft. The Mactan reuse line implies the practical constraint was
  availability on the ground rather than the speed of the arm.

Pigafetta does supply two distance markers, but they are markers of the
battlefield's geometry rather than of the spear. The Spanish waded "more than
two crossbow flights" from the boats to the shore, and later retreated "more
than a good crossbow flight from the shore". A crossbow flight is an imprecise
sixteenth-century unit somewhere in the low hundreds of metres, and the two
uses here are not consistent enough with each other to be treated as a
measurement. They establish that Mactan was fought across a broad, shallow,
open shoreline, which is a scenario fact rather than a weapon statistic.

### Poison

No source consulted associates poison with the thrown spear. Poison is
attributed to arrows and to blowgun darts, and Escalante Alvarado's general
statement that the inhabitants of the Sarangani and Mindanao area "all, as a
rule, possess poisonous herbs, and use them and other poisons in their wars"
does not name spears. The absence should be preserved rather than filled in.

### Armour and shield interaction

The clearest statement is negative and comes from the Luzon campaign of 1570:
the iron-headed lances "become blunt upon striking a fairly good coat of mail".
That is a Spanish combatant reporting on his own mail, not on the shields and
cotton armour already modelled in the game, and it is a partisan report from a
man arguing his own side's competence. Taken at face value it says the
iron-headed lance, thrown or thrust, was checked by European mail — which is
one of the few places any source in this corpus comments on penetration at all.

Against the game's existing defensive equipment there is nothing direct.
Pigafetta records shields being interposed against missiles at Mactan, but the
missiles he names in the same breath are Spanish shot, which passed through
both the shield and the arm behind it. There is no sentence anywhere describing
a Philippine thrown spear meeting a Philippine shield.

### Who carried it

The Mactan account describes more than fifteen hundred men in three divisions
all throwing, with no distinction drawn between an elite and a levy in the act
of throwing. Escalante Alvarado's Sarangani and Mindanao list treats missile
weapons as part of the general equipment set. On this evidence, the thrown
spear was ordinary rather than elite, and no source consulted assigns it to a
rank, a class, or a specialist role.

Regional variation in the *name* is documented (Visayan and Mindanao *bangkaw*,
Tagalog *sibat*, Maguindanao and Sulu *budjak*), and regional variation in the
*point* is documented by Artieda's remark that "in some districts the
lance-points are long and ground to a very fine edge". Regional variation in
throwing practice is not documented in either direction.

### What is not known

- No throwing distance, no volley depth, no number of shafts carried per man.
- Whether the light bamboo throwing spear and the heavy iron-headed lance were
  carried by the same man, by different men, or in different regions. The
  sources describe both classes but never describe a warrior's full loadout.
- Whether throwing was massed and simultaneous or individual and continuous.
  Pigafetta's "so many" describes volume, not organisation.
- Any Philippine term distinguishing a thrown shaft from a thrust one in the
  sixteenth century. Cebuano *bangkaw* carries a throwing sense in modern
  dictionaries, but that is a modern lexicographic reading projected backwards.
- What the fire-hardened stakes were as objects. They are named repeatedly, in
  1521 and again in 1548, but never described.

## 2. Busog — War Bow

**Confidence label: Documented.**

The bow is attested in the same 1521 eyewitness account as the thrown spear, in
the same battle, wounding the same men. It is attested again in the 1540s in
Mindanao, again in 1567 when a physical specimen was shipped to Spain, again in
1573 in a general description, and again around 1590 in the pictorial record.
The weapon class is beyond argument. What is uncertain is its performance, and
the two best sources disagree about that sharply.

### Earliest attestation

Pigafetta, Mactan, 27 April 1521. The bow appears three times in the battle
narrative and twice as a cause of a named casualty:

> They shot so many arrows at us and hurled so many bamboo spears (some of them
> tipped with iron) at the captain-general…

> So many of them charged down upon us that they shot the captain through the
> right leg with a poisoned arrow.

And Pigafetta himself, days later at Cebu:

> I could not go because I was all swollen up by a wound from a poisoned arrow
> which I had received in my face.

Text from
[Blair and Robertson volume XXXIII](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/42884).

Later attestations, in order:

- **c. 1543–1545, Sarangani and Mindanao**, reported by García Descalante
  Alvarado from Lisbon on 7 August 1548: the offensive arms include "bows and
  arrows", and separately a defence is mounted with "small stones, poles,
  arrows, and mangrove cudgels". Blair and Robertson volume II.
- **15 July 1567, Cebu.** Miguel López de Legazpi writes to the prince that he
  is sending weapon specimens, and lists among them "a bow with quiver and
  arrows, all which they use". This is object-level evidence rather than
  description: a sixteenth-century Philippine bow, quiver, and arrows physically
  went to Spain as a set. Blair and Robertson volume II.
- **1573**, Diego de Artieda, *Relation of the Western Islands Called
  Filipinas*, the fullest description in the corpus:

  > They use bows which are very strong and large, and much more powerful than
  > those used by the English. The arrows are made of reeds, the third part
  > consisting of a point made of the hardest wood that can be found. They are
  > not feathered. They poison the arrows with a kind of herb, which in some
  > regions is so deadly that a man dies on the same day when he is wounded;
  > and, no matter how small the wound is, there is no remedy, and the flesh
  > will surely decay unless the antidotal herb, which is found in Luzon, be
  > first applied to the wound.

  Blair and Robertson volume III.
- **1570, Luzon**, reported in the anonymous *Relation of the Conquest of the
  Island of Luzon* (Manila, 20 April 1572), which flatly contradicts Artieda:
  the natives' small weapons are lances, a broad dagger, "and arrows — which
  are weapons of little value". The same document describes the Manila-area
  forts opening fire with "their culverins and a few arrows". Blair and
  Robertson volume III.
- **1582, the Visayas.** Miguel de Loarca's *Relación de las Yslas Filipinas*
  records that the souls of those "killed by arrows (which is considered a very
  honorable death), go to heaven by way of the arch which is formed when it
  rains, and become gods". This is not a weapons description, but it is direct
  evidence that death by arrow carried positive cultural meaning in the
  sixteenth-century Visayas, which is a stronger statement about the bow's
  standing than any performance claim.
  [Blair and Robertson volume V](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/16501).
- **c. 1590–1595, the Boxer Codex.** The Zambal plates depict hunter-warrior
  figures with bows and arrows. Per repository policy the codex guides
  silhouette and colour rather than technical cataloguing.
  [Boxer Codex image category, Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Boxer_Codex);
  the standard scholarly edition is Souza and Turley,
  [*The Boxer Codex: Transcription and Translation*](https://brill.com/view/journals/jjs/4/1/article-p153_22.xml?language=en)
  (Brill, 2016), which was not consulted at page level here.

### A conflict that should be recorded rather than resolved

Artieda in 1573 says the bows were more powerful than English bows. The Luzon
relation, describing 1570, says arrows were weapons of little value. Both are
Spanish, both are within three years of each other, and they cannot both be a
neutral description of the same thing.

The reconciliation that does not require calling either man a liar is that they
are measuring different things in different places. The Luzon writer is
assessing arrows *against Spanish mail*, in the same sentence in which he says
iron lances went blunt on it; he is grading Philippine weapons on their ability
to defeat his own armour, which is a narrow test that most pre-gunpowder
missile weapons fail. Artieda is describing the bow as an object. It is also
worth noting that Artieda's relation is an argument for abandoning the colony,
and that he elsewhere writes that "all the natives of that land are great
liars" — he is not a disinterested observer either.

**Neither statement may be turned into a damage number.** The honest reading is
that the bow was a real and respected weapon whose effect against a
well-armoured European was poor and whose effect against an unarmoured or
lightly armoured opponent, especially a poisoned one, was feared. Pigafetta's
Mactan account supports exactly that shape: the arrow that decided the day hit
Magellan in an unarmoured leg.

### Name check

**The name passes, on a 1521 attestation, and it is the strongest name in this
document.** Pigafetta's Visayan vocabulary of 1521 contains both halves of the
weapon:

> for Bow — bossugh
> for Arrow — oghon

Blair and Robertson's comparative table sets these against the Visayan
dictionaries of Encarnación (1885) and Sanchez de la Rosa (1895):

| English | Pigafetta | Encarnación | Sanchez |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| bow | bossugh | bosog | bosog |
| arrow | oghun | odyong | odiong |

Neither row is marked conjectural. *Bossugh* to *bosog* is a near-identity, and
the gap between attestation and the depicted period is zero years. The word is
also inherited rather than borrowed: it descends from Proto-Malayo-Polynesian
and Proto-Austronesian \**busuʀ*, a root thousands of years older than the
depicted period, and etymologically distinct from the homophonous Tagalog
*busog* meaning "full" or "satiated", which comes from \**bəsuʀ*
([Wiktionary, busog](https://kaikki.org/dictionary/Tagalog/meaning/b/bu/busog.html)).

One note for the tooltip: the Visayan word Pigafetta recorded for the arrow is
*odiong*, not *pana*. *Pana* is Tagalog and is used loosely in modern Filipino
for both the bow and the arrow. If the game ever labels the projectile
separately, *odiong* has the better sixteenth-century Visayan footing, but that
is a separate decision and is not proposed here.

### Player-facing pair label

**Busog — War Bow**, as `HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` already recommends. No
change needed.

### Physical form

From Artieda (1573), the only description in the corpus:

- **Bow:** "very strong and large". No material, no length, no draw weight.
- **Arrow shaft:** reed.
- **Arrow point:** "the hardest wood that can be found", occupying the forward
  third of the arrow's length. Not iron.
- **Fletching:** none. "They are not feathered."
- **Poison:** applied to the point.

From Legazpi (1567): a **quiver** was part of the set, which is the only
evidence for how arrows were carried.

The unfletched reed arrow with a long hardwood foreshaft is the single most
consequential physical detail here, and it cuts against Artieda's own power
claim. An arrow without fletching is not stabilised in flight; it yaws, sheds
velocity, and loses accuracy over distance far faster than a fletched arrow.
A long, dense hardwood foreshaft occupying a third of the arrow moves the
centre of mass forward, which partially compensates by making the shaft
nose-heavy — the same principle that lets a modern unfletched blowgun dart or a
weighted bolt fly straight over a short distance. The combination reads as a
weapon optimised for a heavy, deeply penetrating hit at close to moderate
distance rather than for long-range accuracy.

### Range and rate of fire

**No source gives a range for a Philippine bow.** Every number below is
reasoning from the described construction, and is not a measurement.

- **Effective range: roughly 30 to 60 m.** The upper bound is set by the
  unfletched shaft rather than by the bow's power. A strong self bow will cast
  an arrow far beyond this, but an unstabilised arrow will not group at those
  distances, and the described construction says the makers accepted that
  trade-off. Artieda's "very strong and large" bows argue for the higher end of
  the band and for a flat trajectory within it.
- **Maximum thrown distance: unknown, and deliberately not estimated.** Casting
  distance for an unfletched arrow depends on the arrow's mass and the bow's
  draw, and no source gives either.
- **Rate of fire: roughly 6 to 12 arrows per minute sustained**, that is one
  arrow every 5 to 10 seconds. This is the ordinary band for a self bow drawn
  and loosed by hand from a quiver, and no Philippine source addresses it.
- **The one source-anchored range statement in this document belongs to the
  blowgun, not the bow**, and it is comparative: Artieda writes that blowgun
  arrows have "the same effect, although not with the same range". That fixes
  the *ordering* — bow range exceeds blowgun range — without fixing either
  number. It is the strongest range evidence available for either weapon, and
  it is worth building the game's range values around that ordering rather than
  around any absolute figure.

### Poison

**Documented, and repeatedly.** See section 5 for the consolidated treatment.
For the bow specifically: the arrow that wounded Magellan in the right leg is
called poisoned by an eyewitness in 1521, and Pigafetta describes his own face
wound the same way. Artieda in 1573 describes the poison as applied to the
point and regionally variable in lethality. The Luzon relation of 1572 reports
"an abundance of a certain very poisonous herb which they apply to their
arrows". Escalante Alvarado's 1548 account says the peoples of the Sarangani
and Mindanao area "all, as a rule, possess poisonous herbs, and use them and
other poisons in their wars".

`HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` already carries the correct caution and it holds:
poisoned arrows are attested but must not be presented as universal.

### Armour and shield interaction

The evidence is thin, indirect, and mostly about the wrong armour.

- **Against Spanish mail: poor.** The Luzon relation of 1572 groups arrows with
  the lances that went blunt on "a fairly good coat of mail" and calls them
  weapons of little value. That is the only penetration statement in the
  corpus and it concerns European mail.
- **Against bare flesh: decisive.** Pigafetta records that at Mactan "the
  natives shot only at our legs, for the latter were bare". This is the most
  useful tactical sentence about missile use in the entire record: the
  defenders identified the unarmoured part of an armoured opponent and
  concentrated their missiles on it. A poisoned arrow into a bare leg is what
  killed the expedition's commander.
- **Against the shields and armour already modelled in Hukbo: nothing
  direct.** The corpus describes Philippine shields being interposed against
  *Spanish* shot, and describes Philippine cotton, rattan, wood, buffalo-horn,
  and dogfish-skin armour as existing, but never describes a Philippine arrow
  striking a Philippine shield or corselet. Any interaction the game models
  between the bow and its own shields is a gameplay construction with no
  documentary footing whatsoever, and must be labelled the way the
  tall-hardwood shield multiplier already is.

### Who carried it

- **Mactan, 1521:** undifferentiated. The same mass of more than fifteen
  hundred men is described shooting arrows and throwing spears; Pigafetta
  draws no distinction between archers and spearmen.
- **Sarangani and Mindanao, 1540s:** bows listed as part of the general
  equipment set.
- **Luzon, 1570:** arrows present in fort defence alongside culverins, but
  described as marginal.
- **Zambales, c. 1590:** the Boxer Codex Zambal plates depict a distinct
  hunter-warrior figure with the bow, and secondary literature describes
  European writers characterising the Zambal as excellent archers using
  poisoned arrows. This is the only regional association in the corpus that
  ties the bow to a specific people as a specialism, and it is late-century and
  pictorial. It should not be read as meaning other peoples did not use the
  bow — the 1521, 1548, and 1573 sources say plainly that they did.

**Nothing in the corpus makes the bow elite or common.** It is not associated
with rank, with the *maginoo* or *timawa* strata, or with a specific social
role in any source consulted. Any such association in the game is invented.

### What is not known

- Bow material, length, limb construction, string material, and draw weight.
  None of the five is stated anywhere.
- Arrow length and mass, and therefore anything quantitative about range or
  penetration.
- Whether a distinct archer role existed, or whether every warrior who owned a
  bow also carried a spear and a blade.
- How many arrows a quiver held.
- Whether the bow was used in massed volleys or individually. The Mactan
  account describes volume, not organisation.
- Whether Artieda's comparison to English bows reflects anything he had
  measured or handled. It reads as an impression, and he was not a neutral
  witness.
- The identity of the arrow poison, which is treated in section 5.

## 3. Sumpit — Blowgun

**Confidence label: Documented** for the weapon and its physical form.
**Documented, form uncertain** would be the wrong label here, because Pigafetta
describes the object in more mechanical detail than he gives any other weapon
in the archipelago. What is genuinely uncertain is *where* it was used, and
that is a separate problem addressed below.

### Earliest attestation

Pigafetta, 1521, twice, in two different places, within a few weeks of each
other. Both passages come from the Robertson translation in
[Blair and Robertson volume XXXIII](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/42884).

At Cagayan Sulu, described by Pigafetta as inhabited by people banished from
Brunei:

> They have blowpipes and small quivers at their side, full of arrows and a
> poisonous herb. They have daggers whose hafts are adorned with gold and
> precious gems, spears, bucklers, and small cuirasses of buffalo horn.

At Palawan (*Pulaoan*, *Polaoan*), the fullest description of any ranged weapon
anywhere in the sixteenth-century Philippine record:

> They have blowpipes with thick wooden arrows more than one palmo long, with
> harpoon points, and others tipped with fishbones, and poisoned with an herb;
> while others are tipped with points of bamboo like harpoons and are poisoned.
> At the end of the arrow they attach a little piece of soft wood, instead of
> feathers. At the end of their blowpipes they fasten a bit of iron like a spear
> head; and when they have shot all their arrows they fight with that.

That is, in one paragraph: the ammunition's length, three separate point
materials, the poison, the substitute for fletching, the bayonet fitting at the
muzzle, and the doctrine for what to do when the darts run out.

The only other sixteenth-century mention found is Diego de Artieda's *Relation
of the Western Islands Called Filipinas* (1573), and it is the single most
useful sentence in this entire document for a game:

> Arrows are also discharged through blow-guns with the same effect, although
> not with the same range.

[Blair and Robertson volume III](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/13616).

That sentence establishes two things on sixteenth-century authority: the
blowgun's wounding effect was comparable to the bow's, because the poison was
doing the work in both cases; and its range was shorter. It fixes an ordering
without fixing a number, which is exactly the kind of evidence a deterministic
simulation can honestly build on.

### Name check

**The name passes the hundred-year rule, but on comparative-linguistic evidence
rather than on a dated document, and that distinction has to be recorded in the
evidence tier.**

The negative finding first, because it is the one that matters. The word
*sumpit* appears **nowhere** in Blair and Robertson volumes II, III, V, or
XXXIII. Pigafetta describes the weapon twice and never names it. The Spanish
called it *zarbatana*, an Old Spanish variant of *cerbatana*. No
sixteenth-century document consulted here records a Philippine name for the
blowgun at all.

The positive finding is that the word is nonetheless demonstrably far older
than the depicted period. Robert Blust's *Austronesian Comparative Dictionary*
reconstructs **Proto-Malayo-Polynesian \*sumpit**, glossed "blowpipe,
blowgun", along with Proto-Western-Malayo-Polynesian \**ma-numpit*, "shoot with
a blowpipe" ([ACD cognate set 30701](https://acd.clld.org/cognatesets/30701)).
The Philippine reflexes meaning specifically "blowgun" span the archipelago and
several widely separated subgroups:

| Region | Languages with a "blowgun" reflex |
| --- | --- |
| Northern Luzon | Itawis *súmpit*, Ilokano *sumpít* |
| Central Luzon and Tagalog area | Tagalog *sumpít* |
| Mindoro and Romblon | Hanunóo *sumpít* (here "sling for hurling stones"), Romblomanon *sumpit* |
| Visayas | Masbatenyo *súmpit*, Aklanon *súmpit*, Waray-Waray *sumpít*, Cebuano *sumpít* |
| Mindanao | Binukid *sumpit*, Western Bukidnon Manobo *sumpit*, Mansaka *sompit*, Maguindanao *sumpit*, Maranao *sompit* |
| Sulu and the Sulu Sea | Tausug *sumpit*, Mapun *sumpit* |

A word with a secure Proto-Malayo-Polynesian reconstruction and a reflex
distribution that dense across Luzon, the Visayas, Mindanao, and Sulu was in
Philippine languages millennia before the 1500s. It is not a post-contact
Spanish coinage and it is not a late Malay loan into Philippine languages —
Blust flags loan status only for particular non-Philippine forms such as Wolio
*sumpita*, and notes that even the Banggai loan judgement rests on an unclear
basis. The word therefore does not postdate the depicted period at all, let
alone by a century, and the rule that excluded the panabas is cleared by a wide
margin.

The gap that remains, and should be recorded honestly: **no dated documentary
attestation of *sumpit* in a Philippine language was located.** The obvious
places to find one are Pedro de San Buenaventura's *Vocabulario de la lengua
tagala* (Pila, 1613), whose Spanish-first arrangement would put the entry under
*cerbatana*, and Alonso de Méntrida's *Bocabulario de lengua bisaya, hiligueyna
y haraya* (1637, with a 1628 Manila antecedent). Either would place a written
attestation within a century of the depicted period and would settle the matter
on the same footing as *busog*. Neither could be checked at page level from
this environment, and Wiktionary's *sumpit* entries cite only Blust, with no
dated vocabulario quotation. **This is an open item, not a failure.** The
reconstruction alone is sufficient to clear the rule; a dated entry would
simply upgrade the tier.

One semantic note for anyone writing the tooltip. The root's core meaning is
"propel something through a tube", not "blowgun" specifically. Several reflexes
mean to squirt or spray — Ilokano *sumpít* covers a syringe and an enema,
Cebuano *sumpít* covers a liquid-squirter, Bikol *mag-sumpít* means to spray —
and the Hanunóo reflex means a stone-hurling sling. The blowgun is one
specialisation of a broader root. That is a point of interest, not a problem,
but it means *sumpit* should never be glossed as though it were a proper noun
for one object.

### Player-facing pair label

`HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` currently recommends the bare English label
**Blowgun**, with no Filipino name. On the evidence above that caution is no
longer necessary, and the pair form is available:

**Sumpit — Blowgun**

with the evidence tier in the agent inspector recording that the *weapon* is
Documented from 1521 while the *name* rests on a Proto-Malayo-Polynesian
reconstruction with pan-Philippine reflexes rather than on a
sixteenth-century document. If a reviewer prefers to hold the bare **Blowgun**
label until a dated vocabulario entry is found, that is a defensible and more
conservative position; it is listed in section 9 as a decision for a human.

### Physical form

From Pigafetta (1521), which is the only period source:

- **Tube:** not described. Length, material, and bore are all absent from the
  1521 account.
- **Muzzle fitting:** "a bit of iron like a spear head", making the weapon a
  short spear once the darts were gone. This is the most distinctive documented
  feature and it is the one a spectator could actually see.
- **Dart:** thick wood, "more than one palmo long". A Castilian *palmo* is
  roughly 21 cm, so a dart of roughly 21 to 25 cm or a little more.
- **Dart points:** three variants named — harpoon points, fishbone points, and
  bamboo points shaped like harpoons.
- **Fletching:** none; instead "a little piece of soft wood" at the rear of the
  dart, which is the airtight plug that lets breath pressure act on the dart.
- **Poison:** on the point, in all three variants.
- **Carriage:** at Cagayan Sulu, "small quivers at their side, full of arrows
  and a poisonous herb" — so a hip quiver carrying both the darts and the
  poison stock together.

Later comparative material, which must be marked as later and non-period:
Philippine examples in museum and ethnographic collections are commonly bamboo
or seamless hollowed fan-palm wood, roughly 1.2 to 1.8 m long with a bore of
2 to 3 cm, sometimes assembled from joined sections
([Wikipedia, *Sumpit*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumpit), citing Marinas
2012 and Darmadi 2018). Bornean *sumpitan* of hardwood run 1.2 to 2.4 m with a
bore of 1 to 1.2 cm. None of this is sixteenth-century Philippine evidence and
none of it should be presented as such; it is useful for silhouette and
proportion in the same restricted way the National Museum's later blade
collections already are.

### Range and rate of fire

**One statement is sourced to the period and everything else is not.**

The sourced statement, Artieda 1573: blowgun range is **less than** bow range,
with comparable effect. That ordering is the only thing the game may present as
historically grounded.

Numbers, in descending order of evidentiary quality:

| Figure | Value | Source | Tier |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| Blowgun range relative to bow | shorter | Artieda, 1573 | **Documented** |
| Philippine sumpit maximum range | 46–55 m (150–180 ft) | American observers in the Philippines, 1912, via *The Manila Times*, 24 January 2015 | Later comparative, twentieth-century |
| Batak (northern Palawan) practical range | 20–40 m | secondary ethnographic summary | Later comparative, weak chain |
| Bornean *sumpitan* accurate range | ~25 m | collector and ethnographic literature (van Zonneveld) | Later comparative, not Philippine |
| Bornean *sumpitan* maximum effective range | ~50 m | same | Later comparative, not Philippine |
| Dart muzzle velocity | ~50 m/s (~180 km/h) | same, and modern chronograph testing of commercial blowguns at 150–200 ft/s | Modern, not historical |

**Recommended reading of the above: an effective engagement range of roughly
15 to 30 m, with a hard ceiling somewhere near 45 to 55 m at which hits become
incidental.** That band is consistent with every later figure in the table and
with Artieda's ordering, and it is deliberately narrower than the bow's
proposed 30 to 60 m band so that the documented ordering survives into
gameplay. It is not a measurement.

**Rate of fire: roughly one dart every 4 to 8 seconds.** A blowgun dart must be
selected, poisoned or drawn from the poison store, seated in the bore, and
delivered on a single controlled breath, and the shooter must recover that
breath. This is reasoning from the mechanics of the weapon, not a source. There
is no period statement about how many darts were carried; Pigafetta's phrase
"when they have shot all their arrows" is the only evidence that running out
was a normal and anticipated event, which is itself a useful design signal.

### Poison

**This is the weapon where poison is not optional.** Every single
sixteenth-century mention of the blowgun mentions poison in the same breath.
Pigafetta at Cagayan Sulu: quivers "full of arrows and a poisonous herb".
Pigafetta at Palawan: all three dart types poisoned. Artieda in 1573 makes
"the same effect" the point of comparison with the bow, and the effect he had
just finished describing for arrows was the poison.

The physical reason is straightforward and worth stating in the document
because it determines how the weapon must be modelled: a 21 cm wooden dart
carries almost no kinetic energy. The wound it makes is trivial. The weapon
works entirely by delivering a toxin, which means its lethality is governed by
*hit probability*, not by momentum, and that is precisely why its effective
range is short. A blowgun modelled as a low-damage projectile with no toxin
mechanic is not a blowgun; it is a bad bow.

**What the poison was is not known for the sixteenth-century Philippines.**
Pigafetta and Artieda both say "an herb". No period source names a plant. The
standard modern identification for Bornean and Philippine blowgun poison is the
latex of *Antiaris toxicaria*, known regionally as *upas*, *apo*, or *ipoh*,
whose active agent is the cardiac glycoside antiarin, lethal at roughly
0.1 mg/kg in warm-blooded animals. That identification is well established for
later Bornean and Dayak practice and is carried into the Philippine literature
by continuity, but it is **a Provisional reconstruction for the 1500s
Philippines**, and there is a specific reason for caution: *Antiaris* is a
tree and its poison is a latex, while both period sources say *herb*. That may
be nothing more than a sixteenth-century Italian and a sixteenth-century
Spaniard using a loose word for "plant preparation", but it is a discrepancy
and it should not be smoothed over. **No plant name belongs in player-facing
text.**

An antidote is also documented. Artieda says the flesh "will surely decay
unless the antidotal herb, which is found in Luzon, be first applied to the
wound", and Loarca in 1582 records that Visayan physicians "have a remedy for
every kind of poison, for there are most wonderful antidotal herbs". Two
independent sources, eleven years and one region apart, both report a
countermeasure. That is worth knowing before anyone models poison as an
irreversible death sentence.

### Armour and shield interaction

**No source describes a blowgun dart striking any armour or shield.** Not one.

What can be said from the physics of the weapon, marked clearly as reasoning:
a lightweight wooden dart at roughly 50 m/s will not defeat a wooden shield, a
rattan or hardwood corselet, cotton armour of the kind Escalante Alvarado
describes reaching to the feet with sleeves, or dogfish-skin head armour. It
needs bare skin or a gap. That makes the blowgun the natural counterpart to the
Mactan tactic Pigafetta actually recorded — "the natives shot only at our legs,
for the latter were bare" — and it argues that in a simulation the blowgun
should be the weapon most sharply penalised by any armour at all and most
rewarded by an unarmoured target, far more so than the bow.

That is a design inference. It has no documentary support and must be labelled
provisional wherever it lands in code.

### Who carried it

**This is where the blowgun's evidence is genuinely weak, and where the
repository's regional-generalisation rule bites hardest.**

The sixteenth-century Philippine attestations are geographically narrow:

- **Palawan, 1521.** Pigafetta, direct observation, full description.
- **Cagayan Sulu, 1521.** Pigafetta, direct observation — but he explicitly
  identifies these people as *Moros* banished from Brunei, which makes this an
  observation of a Bornean exile community in Philippine waters rather than of
  an indigenous Philippine tradition.
- **"These islands", 1573.** Artieda, general and undifferentiated, in a
  passage that also describes bows, lances, shields, and armour without
  localising any of them.

**No sixteenth-century source consulted places a blowgun in the Visayas, in
Cebu, at Mactan, in Manila, or in Luzon.** Pigafetta was at Mactan and Cebu and
describes bows, thrown spears, fire-hardened stakes, stones, and mud; he does
not mention a blowgun there. The Luzon campaign accounts of 1570 to 1572
describe lances, daggers, arrows, and culverins; no blowgun. Loarca's Visayan
account of 1582 has no blowgun.

The honest conclusion is that the blowgun in the 1500s record is a **Palawan,
Sulu Sea, and Bornean-contact weapon**, with one late and vague general
statement extending it further. Putting a blowgunner into a Visayan or a
Manila-area battle scenario, without saying so, is exactly the generalisation
`CLAUDE.md` section 7 forbids. If Hukbo's scenarios carry a place tag of the
kind `HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` already proposes (`Mactan — 1521`,
`Manila — c.1570`), the blowgun's tag should read for Palawan or the Sulu Sea,
and the unit should be rare or absent elsewhere.

Nothing in the corpus makes the blowgun elite or common, and nothing associates
it with rank. Later ethnography treats it as a hunting weapon that doubled for
war, and Philippine sources describe small versions as children's toys among
Bukidnon boys — but that is twentieth-century material and says nothing about
1521.

### What is not known

- The length, material, and bore of a sixteenth-century Philippine blowgun.
  Pigafetta describes the darts and the muzzle spearhead in detail and says
  nothing at all about the tube.
- How many darts a quiver held.
- The identity of the poison plant, and whether the "herb" of the sources is
  *Antiaris* latex or something else entirely.
- How quickly the poison acted from a dart, as opposed to from an arrow.
  Artieda's "same effect" is the only comparative statement and it is not a
  timing.
- Whether the blowgun was used in open battle at all, or only in ambush,
  hunting, and raiding. Neither Pigafetta passage describes it being used
  against the Spanish; both are equipment inventories taken during peaceful
  contact.
- Whether the weapon existed in the central Visayas or in Luzon in the 1500s.
  The silence of four eyewitness-adjacent bodies of text is not proof of
  absence, but it is the strongest signal available and it points one way.
- Any sixteenth-century Philippine name for it.

## 4. Imported Arquebus

**Confidence label: Documented, form uncertain.** The presence of matchlock
firearms in local hands in the archipelago is documented three separate times
between roughly 1543 and 1567, and one specimen was physically shipped to
Spain. What is uncertain is the weapon's exact form, and what is emphatically
uncertain is how many there were and who had them. This matches the tier
`HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` already assigns.

### Earliest attestation

**c. 1543–1545, Sarangani and Mindanao**, reported by García Descalante
Alvarado from Lisbon on 7 August 1548. This is the earliest, and it is
carefully hedged in the source itself:

> The offensive arms of the inhabitants of these islands are cutlasses and
> daggers; lances, javelins, and other missile weapons; bows and arrows, and
> culverins. […] In some islands they have small pieces of artillery and a few
> arquebuses.

Note the wording precisely: *some* islands, and *a few* arquebuses. The
earliest source is also the one most explicit that the weapon was uncommon.
[Blair and Robertson volume II](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/13280).

**1565, near Bohol and Butuan.** Legazpi's *Relation of the Voyage to the
Philippine Islands* describes his boats fighting a junk which "had many and
well-made arrows and lances, with a culverin and some muskets", crewed by
forty-five men who "fought well and bravely in their defense" and wounded more
than twenty Spaniards. Legazpi identifies the crew: "Its crew were Burnei
Moros." This is a Bruneian trading vessel armed with a swivel gun and personal
firearms, engaged in Philippine waters — which is precisely the mechanism by
which such weapons reached the archipelago, and precisely why the game's
`IMPORTED` badge is the honest framing. Blair and Robertson volume II.

**15 July 1567, Cebu.** The decisive attestation, because it comes with an
object. Legazpi writes to the prince:

> It seemed to me that your highness would be pleased with specimens of the
> weapons with which these natives fight; accordingly they are bringing to your
> highness a Chinese arquebuse, of which there are some among these natives.
> Although they are very dexterous in handling these guns, when on the sea,
> aboard of their *praus*, they carry them more to terrify than to kill.

Blair and Robertson volume II. A physical Chinese matchlock, taken from local
hands, crossed the Pacific and the Atlantic as a specimen. The sentence that
follows it is the single most important design fact about this weapon and is
discussed below.

### Name check

**Not applicable, and deliberately so.** "Arquebus" is a European term
contemporary with the depicted period, not a cultural identification, so the
pair-form rule and the hundred-year rule do not engage. No sixteenth-century
Philippine name for the weapon was found in any source consulted, and none
should be invented to fill the gap — inventing one would be a far worse
offence against the historical policy than leaving the label in English.

### Player-facing pair label

**Imported Arquebus**, exactly as `HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` already
specifies, with the `IMPORTED` badge. This is one place where the absence of a
Filipino name is itself the historically accurate statement: the weapon
arrived through Chinese and Bornean maritime trade and the sources treat it as
foreign goods in local hands. The badge carries that meaning without a word of
flavour text.

### Physical form

**Almost nothing is documented.** Legazpi calls it a "Chinese arquebuse" and
gives no dimensions, no lock type, no calibre, and no description of the stock
or barrel. The Bruneian junk carried "some muskets", undescribed.

What can be said with reasonable confidence, marked as inference from the
general history of the weapon rather than from any Philippine source: a
mid-sixteenth-century Chinese long gun reaching the Philippines by trade would
be a matchlock — a smoothbore firing a lead ball, ignited by a slow-burning
match cord held in a serpentine and brought to a priming pan by a trigger or
lever. Powder in this period was coarse serpentine rather than corned, which
made performance inconsistent. Ammunition would be loose ball and loose powder,
with a lit match to be kept burning; nothing in the Philippine record says
anything about how much was carried.

The visual language `HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` already proposes — long
timber stock, dark iron barrel, horizontal pose, small glowing matchcord — is
consistent with this and does not overreach. The glowing matchcord in
particular is a genuinely legible cue and is the kind of detail a spectator can
discover without reading source code.

### Range and rate of fire

**No Philippine source gives either.** The figures below are the general
performance envelope of a sixteenth-century matchlock and are drawn from the
history-of-firearms literature, principally Bert Hall's *Weapons and Warfare
in Renaissance Europe* and Tonio Andrade's *The Gunpowder Age* as summarised in
[Wikipedia's *Arquebus* article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arquebus). They
describe European and Chinese matchlocks in general, not any Philippine
example.

- **Sustained battlefield rate of fire: roughly 1 shot per minute.** Two to
  three shots per minute is achievable by a skilled operator in ideal
  conditions; the loading cycle involves between twelve and twenty-eight
  discrete steps, and damp powder, rain, fouling, or a match cord that has gone
  out push the interval past sixty seconds. For a simulation with a
  sixteenth-century setting, one shot per minute is the defensible planning
  figure and anything faster needs justifying.
- **Effective range against an individual: roughly 50 to 100 m.** Modern
  testing suggests hit rates of 10 to 20 per cent against a man-sized target at
  50 m, falling to roughly 2 per cent at 100 m.
- **Nominal "deadly" range: several hundred metres, and practically
  worthless.** Contemporaries said so at the time. Sir John Smythe observed in
  1590 that arquebusiers firing at extreme distances rarely seemed to hit
  anything.

Two period Philippine data points bear on this indirectly.

First, Pigafetta at Mactan: "The musketeers and crossbowmen shot from a
distance for about a half-hour, but uselessly." Thirty minutes of Spanish
arquebus and crossbow fire produced no useful result, against a target that
"would never stand still, but leaped hither and thither". That is a
sixteenth-century eyewitness recording, in the Philippines, that matchlock fire
at range against mobile targets did not work.

Second, "arquebus-shot" was used as a casual distance unit in these documents.
Loarca in 1582 repeatedly places one island "about two arquebus-shots" from
another. Like the crossbow flight, it is imprecise and cannot be converted into
a number, but its everyday use as a short-distance measure is a reminder that
these writers thought of the arquebus's reach in hundreds of metres rather than
tens.

### Poison

Not applicable, and not attested. No source associates poison with firearms.

### Armour and shield interaction

**This is the one weapon in this document with a genuinely documented
armour interaction, and it comes from an eyewitness.** Pigafetta, Mactan, 1521:

> The musketeers and crossbowmen shot from a distance for about a half-hour,
> but uselessly; for the shots only passed through the shields which were made
> of thin wood and the arms [of the bearers].

The natural reading is that the ball penetrated the thin-wood shield and the
arm holding it and went no further — the shield plus the limb behind it
absorbed the shot at that range. Pigafetta offers this as the *explanation* for
why the fire was useless, which is the sense the sentence has to carry.

Two cautions before this becomes a game rule. It is a single sentence written
by a man explaining a defeat, and "uselessly" may be doing more work than
"only passed through" — he may mean the fire failed to break the charge rather
than that it failed to kill anyone. And the shields in question are described
as thin wood, which is not obviously the same object as the tall hardwood
shield Hukbo already models from the Boxer Codex Cagayan figure.

Pigafetta also records the behavioural response, which is separately useful:
"When our muskets were discharged, the natives would never stand still, but
leaped hither and thither, covering themselves with their shields." Evasion
plus shield cover, against firearms specifically — the same two-channel defence
that `WEAPON_CLASH_1500s.md` identifies as the only documented defensive
behaviour in the corpus.

### Who carried it

**Rare, maritime, and skilled.** Every source agrees on rarity and none
contradicts it.

- Escalante Alvarado, 1540s: "in some islands", "a few arquebuses". Sarangani
  and Mindanao.
- Legazpi, 1565: aboard a Bruneian junk in Philippine waters, alongside a
  culverin — foreign crew, foreign vessel, trade route.
- Legazpi, 1567: "some among these natives", in the Cebu area, and specifically
  **aboard *praus* at sea**. He calls the users "very dexterous in handling
  these guns", which is a compliment about skill from a man with every
  professional reason to be dismissive.

The Manila-area campaign accounts of 1570 to 1572 describe local forces using
**culverins** in fortifications and boats, not personal arquebuses. That
distinction should be preserved: the crew-served bronze piece and the shoulder
arm are different weapons with different distributions, and the culverin is
deferred (see section 7).

**Legazpi's "more to terrify than to kill" is the design brief for this
weapon.** It is a sixteenth-century eyewitness stating that the firearm's
battlefield function, as locally employed, was psychological rather than
lethal. A game that models the imported arquebus purely as a damage number has
discarded the only statement of purpose any source gives it. If Hukbo ever
gains a morale model, this is the weapon that should have argued for it; while
it does not, the honest fallback is to make the arquebus rare, loud, visually
distinctive, and unimpressive in raw attrition, which is what the sources
actually describe.

No source associates the arquebus with a social rank. The maritime context —
*praus*, junks, coastal trade — implies access through trade wealth rather than
through birth, but that is an inference and no source states it.

### What is not known

- How many arquebuses existed anywhere in the archipelago at any date. "Some"
  and "a few" is the whole of the evidence.
- What kind of Chinese matchlock it was, and therefore its calibre, barrel
  length, and lock mechanism.
- Whether powder and ball were locally made, locally traded, or imported with
  the gun. This matters enormously for whether the weapon could be used more
  than a handful of times, and nothing addresses it.
- Whether any local force ever used firearms in a land battle, as opposed to
  aboard a *prau*. Legazpi's phrasing points at sea use specifically.
- Whether "more to terrify than to kill" is a statement about local doctrine or
  a Spanish commander's condescension. It could be either and the text does not
  settle it.
- Anything at all about maintenance, match cord supply, or performance in the
  tropical wet season, which for a matchlock is the question that decides
  whether it works.

## 5. Poison, treated as its own question

Poison is the thread that connects three of the four weapons, and it is better
handled once than four times.

### What is documented

Five independent sixteenth-century statements, spanning fifty years and four
regions:

| Date | Source | Region | Statement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1521 | Pigafetta | Mactan, Cebu | Magellan "shot… through the right leg with a poisoned arrow"; Pigafetta himself wounded in the face by a poisoned arrow |
| 1521 | Pigafetta | Cagayan Sulu | quivers "full of arrows and a poisonous herb" |
| 1521 | Pigafetta | Palawan | all three blowgun dart types "poisoned with an herb" |
| c. 1543–45 (written 1548) | Escalante Alvarado | Sarangani, Mindanao | "They all, as a rule, possess poisonous herbs, and use them and other poisons in their wars" |
| 1570 (written 1572) | anonymous | Luzon, Manila area | "an abundance of a certain very poisonous herb which they apply to their arrows" |
| 1573 | Artieda | general | poison on arrow points, "in some regions so deadly that a man dies on the same day when he is wounded"; blowgun darts to the same effect |

That is as well attested as anything in this document. **Poison on projectiles
in the sixteenth-century Philippines is Documented.**

### What is not documented

- **The plant.** Not one source names it. Pigafetta says "herb", Artieda says
  "a kind of herb", the Luzon relation says "a certain very poisonous herb".
  Every identification in the modern literature is a back-projection.
- **Whether it was universal.** Artieda explicitly makes it regional — deadly
  "in some regions", implying not everywhere and not equally. Escalante
  Alvarado's "all, as a rule" is a generalisation about the Sarangani and
  Mindanao area only, and is exactly the kind of sweeping colonial statement
  the repository policy says to discount.
- **The onset time.** Artieda's "dies on the same day" is the only timing in
  the corpus and it is a maximum-case regional claim, not a norm.

### The *Antiaris* identification

The standard modern identification for Southeast Asian blowgun poison is the
latex of *Antiaris toxicaria* — *upas*, *apo*, or *ipoh* — whose active agent
is the cardiac glycoside antiarin, lethal to warm-blooded animals at roughly
0.1 mg per kilogram of body mass. This is well established for later Bornean
and Dayak practice and is carried into the Philippine literature by continuity
of tradition.

**For the sixteenth-century Philippines this is a Provisional
reconstruction**, and there is a specific reason to keep it at that tier
rather than promoting it: *Antiaris* is a tree and its poison is a latex, while
every period source says *herb*. That discrepancy may be nothing — two European
laymen using a loose word — but it is unresolved, and a plant name in
player-facing text would be asserting an identification no source makes.
**Keep the plant out of the interface.**

### The antidote

Two independent sources describe a countermeasure, which is a detail almost
always dropped from popular accounts.

- Artieda, 1573: the flesh "will surely decay unless the antidotal herb, which
  is found in Luzon, be first applied to the wound".
- Loarca, 1582, the Visayas: there are "good physicians, who cure diseases with
  medicinal herbs; especially they have a remedy for every kind of poison, for
  there are most wonderful antidotal herbs".

Two writers, eleven years apart, two regions, both reporting that the poison
was treatable. Before anyone models poison as an irreversible sentence, this
belongs on the record.

### The design consequence, stated as a design consequence

The blowgun is the weapon that *requires* a toxin mechanic, because its dart
carries almost no kinetic energy and the wound it makes is otherwise trivial.
The bow does not require one — a heavy hardwood-pointed arrow from a strong bow
is a lethal object on its own — but the sources say it usually had one anyway.
The thrown spear is not associated with poison in any source and should not
acquire one.

`HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` already forbids "universal poison effects for
every bow or blowgun" in its avoid list. That constraint is correct and the
evidence gathered here strengthens rather than weakens it. If Hukbo models
poison at all, it should be a per-agent or per-scenario property with a
regional basis, not a flat property of a weapon class.

## 6. Projectiles against the shields and armour already in the game

Hukbo already models a tall hardwood shield, a narrow breast-high shield, and
cotton, rattan, hide, and dark-wood armour. Here is the complete set of
sixteenth-century statements bearing on how projectiles met those things — and
it is a short list, which is itself the finding.

**What the sources actually say:**

1. **A Philippine shield is interposed against missiles, deliberately and with
   footwork.** Pigafetta, Mactan 1521: "When our muskets were discharged, the
   natives would never stand still, but leaped hither and thither, covering
   themselves with their shields." Documented.
2. **A thin-wood shield plus the arm behind it stopped a Spanish ball at
   range.** Pigafetta, same passage: the shots "only passed through the shields
   which were made of thin wood and the arms [of the bearers]". Documented,
   with the reading caveat given in section 4.
3. **Philippine iron lance points went blunt on Spanish mail, and arrows were
   rated of little value against it.** The Luzon relation of 1572. Documented
   as a Spanish combatant's assessment; not neutral.
4. **Missiles were aimed at unarmoured body parts by choice.** Pigafetta,
   Mactan: "The natives shot only at our legs, for the latter were bare."
   Documented, and the single most useful tactical sentence in the corpus.
5. **Philippine armour existed in variety and covered a lot.** Escalante
   Alvarado, Sarangani and Mindanao, 1540s: "cotton corselets reaching to the
   feet and with sleeves; corselets made of wood and buffalo horn; and cuirasses
   made of bamboo and hard wood, which entirely cover them. Armor for the head is
   made of dogfish-skin, which is very tough." Artieda, 1573: "armor consisting
   of cotton-lined blankets, and others of rattan. Some wear corselets, made of
   a very hard black wood resembling ebony." Pigafetta's Visayan vocabulary of
   1521 records *baluti* for "quilted garments used for fighting" and *calassan*
   for "shields". All Documented.
6. **Shield dimensions, from Artieda 1573:** "shields, breast-high, and little
   more than half a *vara* wide" — roughly 0.42 m wide, which corroborates the
   narrow breast-high shield already in the game's visual vocabulary.

**What no source says, anywhere:**

- Nothing describes a Philippine arrow, dart, or thrown spear striking a
  Philippine shield or a Philippine corselet. Not one sentence. Every
  penetration statement in the corpus is about a European weapon against a
  Philippine shield, or a Philippine weapon against European mail.
- Nothing gives a shield's thickness, layer count, or construction beyond
  Pigafetta's "thin wood".
- Nothing distinguishes the tall shield's performance from the narrow shield's.

**The consequence for the game is the same one `WEAPON_CLASH_1500s.md` already
reached for melee, and it should be restated rather than rediscovered:** any
interception, deflection, or damage-reduction number Hukbo assigns for a
projectile against its own shields is a gameplay tuning value with no
evidentiary confidence whatsoever, and it must be marked `PROVISIONAL` in
source and in tests exactly as the tall-hardwood shield multiplier already is.

The two things that *can* be grounded, and should be, are directional rather
than numeric:

- **Aiming at the unarmoured part is documented behaviour.** If Hukbo's
  body-part hit-location system can express a missile bias toward legs and
  other uncovered locations against an armoured target, that is a
  source-anchored feature and a spectator-discoverable one.
- **The shield is an active instrument used with footwork, not a static
  wall.** Documented against missiles specifically, in 1521, which is a
  stronger footing than the melee case has.

## 7. What was deliberately excluded, and why

### Bronze verso, small culverin, and the name *lantaka*

**Excluded because it is crew-served, and deferred by existing policy.**

The evidence for it is strong. Escalante Alvarado lists culverins among the
offensive arms in the Sarangani and Mindanao area in the 1540s. The Manila-area
forts in 1570 opened fire with "their culverins", and one fort yielded "ten or
twelve culverins" when taken. Legazpi wrote from Panay on 25 July 1570 that he
was sending "two bronze culverins [*versos*] made by the Moros of this land",
as evidence of local metalworking skill. Artieda in 1573 wrote that Moros
trading with the Japanese and Chinese carried "bronze culverins, so excellent
and well cast, that I have never seen their equal anywhere".

That is a documented, locally *manufactured* weapon with a better production
footing than the imported arquebus has. It is excluded here for two reasons
that have nothing to do with the evidence: the brief fixes four weapons, and
`HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` already reserves the bronze verso "for a later
crew-served or emplacement system". A weapon that needs a crew, a mount, and a
recoil footprint is a different simulation problem from a weapon one agent
carries.

**On the name:** *lantaka* appears **zero times** in Blair and Robertson
volumes II, III, V, and XXXIII. The sixteenth-century sources say *verso* and
*culebrina*. The word is Malay in origin, from *lantak* meaning to ram or tamp
down, and the surviving catalogued objects in the Metropolitan Museum, the UK
collections, and the Sea Research Society holdings are consistently dated
seventeenth to nineteenth century. `HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` already says
to use **Bronze Verso** or **Small Culverin** before adopting *lantaka*, and
this research supports that instruction rather than overturning it. Whether
*lantaka* would clear the hundred-year rule is genuinely unresolved and should
be settled properly before the name is used, not assumed either way.

### Thrown stones, mud, and fire-hardened stakes as separate weapons

**Excluded as a scope decision, not an evidence problem.** They are extremely
well documented — "pointed stakes hardened with fire, stones, and mud" at
Mactan in 1521, "small stones, poles, arrows, and mangrove cudgels as large
around as the arm, the ends sharpened and hardened in the fire" at Sarangani in
the 1540s — and they were plainly a real part of how these fights were fought.
They are not on the four-weapon list, and folding them into the *bangkaw* would
misrepresent both. They are recorded in section 1 as context and would make a
legitimate future entry.

### The "Hardened Javelin" already in the existing roster

**Deliberately not merged into the *bangkaw*.**
`HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` carries a separate roster entry for a
fire-hardened throwing spear or javelin, distinct from the long iron-tipped
lance, on Pigafetta's own distinction between javelins, fire-hardened spears,
and iron-pointed lances. This document treats the *bangkaw* in its thrown role
and does not touch that entry. Anyone implementing from this document should be
aware that the existing roster already contains a thrown-spear-shaped weapon,
and that the relationship between the two is a design question this research
does not answer.

### *Pana* as a name for the bow

**Excluded in favour of *busog*.** *Pana* is Tagalog, is used loosely in modern
Filipino for both the bow and the arrow, and has no sixteenth-century
attestation located here. *Busog* has a direct 1521 Visayan attestation in
Pigafetta's own word list plus a Proto-Austronesian reconstruction. There is no
reason to prefer the weaker name.

### The panabas, and anything else failing the hundred-year rule

**No weapon in this document failed the hundred-year rule.** All three Filipino
names proposed — *bangkaw*, *busog*, *sumpit* — clear it, two of them on
sixteenth-century documentary attestation and the third on a
Proto-Malayo-Polynesian reconstruction with pan-Philippine reflexes. The
panabas is not relevant here because it is not a ranged weapon; it is mentioned
only because it is the precedent that makes the rule load-bearing.

### Community and enthusiast sources, and what was done with them

The brief asked that these be consulted and labelled, and they were.

- **Consulted and used, clearly labelled as later or secondary:**
  [FMA Pulse](https://fmapulse.com/fma-corner/fma-corner-sumpit-filipino-blowgun/),
  *The Manila Times* on the sumpit (24 January 2015, source of the 1912
  American observers' range figure),
  [KajoMag on the Bornean sumpit](https://kajomag.com/5-things-you-should-know-about-sumpit-or-blowpipe-of-borneo/),
  [Mandarin Mansion's sumpitan glossary](https://www.mandarinmansion.com/glossary/sumpitan),
  and BladeForums threads on spear and staff terminology. These supplied
  construction detail, modern range figures, and the observation that FMA
  practitioners disagree among themselves about whether *bangkaw* means a spear
  or a staff. **Nothing from any of them was promoted to Documented**, and no
  number from any of them appears in this document without its tier attached.
- **Consulted and rejected:** Grokipedia pages on the sumpit, the sibat, the
  lantaka, and the arquebus. These are AI-generated and are not citable; where
  their claims matched a real source the real source was used instead, and
  where they did not — for example the assertion of ninth-century Borobudur
  blowgun reliefs and tenth-century Arabic references — the claims were
  dropped. `WEAPON_CLASH_1500s.md` already excludes AI-generated encyclopedia
  pages as unreliable and that exclusion is applied here.
- **Could not be consulted:** Reddit. The search tooling in this environment
  reports that `reddit.com` is not accessible to its user agent, so
  r/FilipinoHistory and the r/Philippines history threads named in the brief
  were **not** checked. That is a gap, not a finding, and it is listed in
  section 9.
- Commercial dealer listings and sword retailers were treated as evidence about
  surviving objects and their dating only, never about the sixteenth century.

### Everything about Philippine metallurgy

**Excluded outright.** `WEAPON_CLASH_1500s.md` already bans hardness numbers,
chip probabilities, and "rivals Toledo" claims about pre-colonial Philippine
steel on the grounds that no metallurgical study of a dated sixteenth-century
Philippine object exists. That ban applies with equal force to arrow points,
lance heads, and gun barrels. Nothing of the kind appears above.

## 9. Open questions needing a human decision

1. **Should the blowgun get the pair-form label *Sumpit — Blowgun*, or stay at
   the bare English *Blowgun*?** The name clears the hundred-year rule on a
   Proto-Malayo-Polynesian reconstruction with reflexes across Luzon, the
   Visayas, Mindanao, and Sulu, but no dated documentary attestation was found.
   That is a weaker footing than *busog* or *bangkaw* and a reviewer could
   reasonably want the conservative option. This document recommends the pair
   form with the tier recorded, but the decision is not the researcher's.
2. **A dating discrepancy in `HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md` that should be
   checked by a person.** That document attributes to "a 1569 relation" the
   descriptions of long lances with broad iron or steel points, and of large
   bows with reed shafts, hardwood points, and unfeathered arrows. Reading
   Blair and Robertson volume III directly, both passages are in **Diego de
   Artieda's *Relation of the Western Islands Called Filipinas*, which Blair
   and Robertson date to 1573.** The 1569 document in that volume — Legazpi's
   undated *Relation of the Filipinas Islands and of the Character and
   Conditions of their Inhabitants* — contains no weapons description at all.
   This research document uses 1573. Correcting the other file is outside this
   task's scope and needs a decision.
3. **Where may a blowgunner appear?** No sixteenth-century source places a
   blowgun in the Visayas, at Mactan, in Cebu, in Manila, or in Luzon. The
   attestations are Palawan and Cagayan Sulu in 1521, plus one vague general
   statement in 1573. If Hukbo's scenarios carry place tags, restricting the
   blowgun to Palawan and Sulu Sea scenarios is the historically defensible
   choice, and making it available everywhere is the generalisation
   `CLAUDE.md` section 7 forbids. This is a gameplay-availability decision with
   a historical constraint attached.
4. **How should "more to terrify than to kill" be honoured without a morale
   model?** Legazpi's 1567 statement is the only sixteenth-century description
   of what the imported arquebus was *for*, and it describes a psychological
   effect Hukbo currently has no mechanism to express. Options are to keep the
   weapon rare and low-attrition and accept the loss, to give it a
   presentation-only shock treatment, or to let it argue for the morale model
   that `CLAUDE.md` section 9 currently defers. All three are design decisions.
5. **Should poison be modelled at all, and at what granularity?** It is well
   documented, it is explicitly regional in the sources, and it is the entire
   reason the blowgun works. But it is also a new state-carrying mechanic on
   `Hukbo.Core` agents with determinism implications, and an antidote is
   documented in two independent sources. A per-agent or per-scenario property
   is defensible; a flat weapon-class property is not.
6. **Reddit and the Filipino-history community forums were not checked**,
   because `reddit.com` is not reachable by this environment's search agent.
   If community consensus on any of these four weapons matters to the design,
   someone with a browser needs to look. Nothing in this document depends on
   it.
7. **Two page-level source checks would materially upgrade this document.**
   The first is Souza and Turley's Brill edition of the Boxer Codex, for the
   actual Spanish text of the Zambal and Cagayan weapon descriptions rather
   than the pictorial summary used here. The second is San Buenaventura's
   *Vocabulario de la lengua tagala* (1613) or Méntrida's *Bocabulario de
   lengua bisaya* (1637) under the headword *cerbatana*, which would settle
   question 1 outright. Neither was reachable at page level from this
   environment. William Henry Scott's *Barangay* (1994) remains unread at page
   level here, as `WEAPON_CLASH_1500s.md` also records.
