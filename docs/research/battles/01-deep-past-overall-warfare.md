# Deep Past, Depth 1: Overall Warfare

## Purpose

This document establishes the broadest defensible model of conflict in the
Philippine archipelago before sustained Spanish observation. It answers what
warfare could accomplish, where it occurred, how scale probably varied, and
which claims remain beyond the evidence.

It does **not** describe one timeless "Filipino way of war." The archipelago
contained different communities, languages, political systems, environments,
and historical trajectories. The best evidence is unevenly concentrated in
Batanes, the Tanjay region of Negros Oriental, the Butuan area, and excavated
burial sites. Those cases cannot stand automatically for Luzon, the Visayas,
Mindanao, and the Sulu zone as wholes.

## Chronological boundary

The "deep past" track has three working layers:

| Layer | Approximate dates | What the evidence can contribute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neolithic | about 2200-500 BCE, varying by region | Settlement, exchange, water mobility, and some tool traditions; almost no direct evidence for organized fighting. |
| Metal Age | about 500 BCE-1000 CE | Metal tools and spearheads, differentiated burials, wider exchange, and emerging social inequality; still little direct evidence for battles. |
| Protohistoric / late pre-contact | about 1000-1521 CE | Settlement hierarchy, imported prestige goods, boats, fortified places, and a small number of violent or unusual burials. This layer has the strongest deep-past warfare evidence. |

These are analytical ranges, not synchronized archipelago-wide stages. The
[National Museum's archaeological collections](https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/our-collections/archaeology/)
use comparable broad periods, while the [Oxford Handbook overview of
Philippine prehistory](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/42054/chapter-abstract/355848376)
emphasizes the regional and chronological complexity behind any national
periodization.

## Evidence labels used here

- **Attested** — directly demonstrated by relevant material remains or a
  historical text created before 1521.
- **Strong reconstruction** — supported by multiple forms of evidence or by a
  sustained archaeological pattern, but not directly witnessed.
- **Plausible inference** — mechanically and contextually consistent, but not
  directly demonstrated.
- **Unknown or unsupported** — the present evidence does not establish it.

Archaeological objects attest that something existed, not every way it was
used. A spearhead does not preserve a formation; a boat does not identify a
warship; a fortified hill does not record the order of defenders; and a
traumatic injury does not by itself identify battle, murder, execution, or
ritual killing.

## Core conclusions

### 1. Conflict existed, but its frequency changed through time and place

**Attested, locally:** Fifteenth- to sixteenth-century burials at Tanjay,
Negros Oriental include detached crania and skeletal injuries consistent with
violent death. Laura Lee Junker interprets these remains, together with
settlement and mortuary evidence, as signs of intensified raiding and warfare
in a late pre-contact maritime chiefdom
([Junker 1999, pp. 24-58](https://www.jstor.org/stable/29792432)).

**Disputed interpretation:** The violent or unusual deposit is not a preserved
battle report. Bonn Aure argues that the Tanjay mass burial can sustain
alternative ritual interpretations and that detached crania need not prove
that all people in the grave died in one raid
([Aure 2004, pp. 161-177](https://www.jstor.org/stable/29792554)). Hukbo
research should therefore say "evidence of interpersonal violence and
contested head-taking or mortuary practice," not "an excavated battlefield
massacre."

**Strong reconstruction, limited to the sampled record:** Junker found that
available Metal Age and early second-millennium cemetery samples did not show
the same observed pattern of traumatic death as the late Tanjay burials. She
argued for increasing scale and intensity of conflict in some lowland maritime
trading chiefdoms toward contact
([Junker 1999](https://www.jstor.org/stable/29792432);
[Junker, *Raiding, Trading, and Feasting*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt6wr1cq)).

**Unknown or unsupported:** The absence of reported trauma in earlier samples
does not establish an earlier peaceful age. Preservation, cremation, secondary
burial, incomplete excavation, selective publication, and injuries that leave
no mark on bone all reduce visibility. Nor does Tanjay prove that warfare
intensified everywhere at the same time.

### 2. Late pre-contact conflict was embedded in political economy

**Strong reconstruction, strongest for coastal and riverine chiefdoms from the
late first millennium onward:** Archaeology shows increasing differences in
grave goods, settlement rank, craft production, and access to imported
prestige goods. The [National Museum's burial-goods
overview](https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/2021/11/23/burial-goods/) notes
greater stratification in Protohistoric-period burials than in earlier Metal
Age samples. Junker's synthesis connects this unequal access to leaders who
built personalized coalitions through exchange, feasting, trade, and war
([Junker 1999](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt6wr1cq);
[Junker and Niziolek 2010, pp. 17-53](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/289944019_Food_Preparation_and_Feasting_in_the_Household_and_Political_Economy_of_Pre-hispanic_Philippine_Chiefdoms)).

Within that model, raiding could:

- seize people whose labor or ransom had value;
- acquire food stores, metal, ornaments, and imported prestige goods;
- damage a rival's trading position or ability to attract followers;
- answer an earlier attack or death;
- display a leader's capacity and redistribute captured wealth; and
- reinforce or renegotiate alliances.

These purposes are **strong reconstructions for some late pre-contact
chiefdoms**, because Junker's model combines archaeology with Chinese records,
early-contact accounts, and later ethnography. They are not all directly
visible in an excavated deposit, and they should not be applied to every
community or earlier millennium.

### 3. Capturing mobile wealth was often more useful than annexing land

**Strong reconstruction, not a universal rule:** Junker argues that in many
Philippine chiefdom settings labor, portable wealth, and access to exchange
were more politically valuable than permanent territorial occupation. This
helps explain raiding as a recurring strategic form rather than assuming every
conflict aimed to hold a continuous frontier
([Junker 1999, especially the warfare synthesis in chapter
12](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt6wr1cq)).

This model fits a landscape of dispersed settlements and shifting personal
alliances, but it has limits:

- agricultural land and resource locations could still matter;
- controlling a river mouth, harbor, or settlement could affect trade without
  resembling a modern territorial conquest;
- fortified residence or refuge sites imply defense of places and people; and
- later-contact evidence must not be used to turn a broad economic model into
  a universal rule about pre-contact war aims.

**Unknown or unsupported:** No source permits a fixed percentage of "raids"
versus "battles," or proves that territorial conquest never occurred.

### 4. Water was a strategic network, not empty space between battlefields

**Attested capability:** The Butuan boats, found in an old river system and
dated by the National Museum to the eighth through tenth centuries CE, are
material evidence for substantial local boatbuilding and riverine/coastal
mobility
([National Museum 2022](https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/2022/10/24/preserving-our-heritage-towards-the-future-of-our-museums/)).
Their edge-joined, lashed-lug construction belongs to a broader Southeast Asian
technical tradition. Martin Roderick Stead's archaeological synthesis
distinguishes this construction evidence from the later historical and
ethnographic records used to discuss sixteenth-century boats
([Stead 2018](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/421115/)).

**Strong reconstruction:** In an archipelago whose important settlements often
occupied coasts and rivers, boats allowed people, food, valuables, and armed
groups to move between communities. Maritime mobility therefore shaped who
could reach whom, how quickly warnings and reinforcements could travel, and
which settlements were exposed.

**Unknown or unsupported:** Archaeology has not demonstrated that a specific
Butuan boat was a warship, how warriors were arranged aboard it, whether boats
fought in lines or swarms, or what force it could carry into battle. Boat
capacity is evidence of mobility and coordination, not an attested naval
formation.

### 5. Defensive landscapes were regionally specific

**Attested, Batanes:** Archaeological and ethnohistorical study identifies
*ijang* as elevated, fortified settlement or refuge places in Batanes.
Lacsina's University of the Philippines thesis assembles archaeological,
historical, and oral evidence while explicitly noting the scarcity and mixed
date of the non-archaeological sources
([Lacsina 2009](https://pages.upd.edu.ph/sites/default/files/asp/files/lacsina_thesis.pdf)).
The broader, open-access Batanes archaeological sequence covers roughly four
millennia and supplies the necessary regional chronology
([Bellwood and Dizon 2013](https://doi.org/10.22459/TA40.12.2013)).

**Strong reconstruction, Batanes only:** Height, restricted approaches, and
modified settlement surfaces could improve observation, delay an attacker,
protect inhabitants, and turn an assault into a difficult uphill approach.

**Unknown or unsupported:** Batanes *ijang* do not prove an archipelago-wide
fortification style. They also do not reveal the number of defenders, a wall
manning drill, weapon distribution, or exact siege procedure.

### 6. Language preserves concepts, not battle narratives

**Attested linguistic reconstruction:** The Austronesian Comparative Dictionary
reconstructs Proto-Western Malayo-Polynesian `*ayaw`, "raid, go headhunting,"
from cognates that include Kankanaey and Manobo forms
([ACD `*ayaw`](https://acd.clld.org/cognatesets/24930)). It also reconstructs
Proto-Philippine `*bákal`, "spear used in warfare; throw a spear at someone,"
although the editors flag semantic uncertainty around forms also meaning
"iron"
([ACD `*bákal`](https://acd.clld.org/cognatesets/33998)).

**Strong reconstruction:** Raiding and spear-related fighting were old enough
concepts to leave inherited vocabulary across multiple language branches.

**Unknown or unsupported:** A reconstructed word cannot date a specific
campaign, locate a practice in every island, establish frequency, or recover
formation, command, ritual, or technique. The ACD itself warns that some
proposed Proto-Philippine reconstructions have weak support or may reflect
borrowing, so each cognate set must be evaluated rather than treating the
dictionary as a list of directly observed practices
([ACD methodology](https://acd.clld.org/about)).

## Strategic settings

| Setting | Defensible claim | Evidence label | Important limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Coast and river mouth | Water access connected settlements to exchange and made rapid intercommunity movement possible. | **Strong reconstruction** | No preserved order of naval battle. |
| River corridor | Boats could move people and goods inland or downstream; settlements along waterways could monitor access. | **Strong reconstruction** | Reconnaissance and interception procedures are unknown. |
| Fortified hill or refuge | Elevated *ijang* gave some Batanes communities a defensible place. | **Attested**, regional | Not a universal Philippine fort type. |
| Settlement edge | Late Tanjay evidence is consistent with violent attacks affecting inhabitants. | **Plausible inference**, disputed | The mass grave's cause remains contested. |
| Forest or interior route | Concealment, broken visibility, and restricted paths would affect movement. | **Plausible inference** | No deep-past Philippine source describes a military ambush drill. |
| Open ground | Groups could physically assemble and fight there. | **Plausible inference** | No evidence establishes a standard large-field formation. |

## A cautious campaign model

The following is a **planning envelope**, not a recovered doctrine:

1. **Political preparation:** a leader attracts or negotiates support through
   personal ties, exchange, and shared interest.
2. **Concentration:** participants gather at a settlement, landing place, or
   boat group.
3. **Approach:** movement follows navigable water, coast, ridge, or local path.
4. **Decision:** the force attacks, threatens, attempts seizure, or withdraws
   according to resistance and opportunity.
5. **Dispersal and distribution:** participants return, redistribute portable
   gains, care for losses, and renegotiate status.

Only the political-economic and mobility constraints have substantial support.
The sequence is a useful way to ask questions; it is **not** evidence that every
campaign followed five formal phases.

## What a "huge battlefield" should mean in deep-past research

The phrase should not automatically evoke two uniform armies arrayed in
European-style lines.

**Plausible inference:** A large confrontation, if one occurred, was more
likely a temporary coalition of contingents connected to different leaders
than a permanent centralized army. Its battlefield footprint could be
irregular because groups arrived by separate boats or paths and because local
terrain broke lines of sight.

**Unknown or unsupported:**

- exact force sizes;
- permanent regiments or standardized ranks;
- a national or pan-regional doctrine;
- named formations;
- regular depth and frontage;
- uniform shield walls, spear blocks, missile screens, or reserves;
- a fixed command hierarchy above allied leaders; and
- standardized victory conditions, surrender procedures, or casualty
  thresholds.

For deeper analysis of what group geometry can safely be modeled, see
[Depth 3](03-deep-past-formations-and-tactics.md).

## Regional coverage and gaps

| Region | Deep-past evidence used here | Coverage status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Northernmost Luzon / Batanes | Long archaeological sequence and *ijang* studies. | Strong for settlement and defensive landscape; weak for fighting behavior. |
| Central and southern Luzon | Burial and trade evidence exists, including Santa Ana and Calatagan, but this file has no direct battle context. | Major tactical gap. |
| Central Visayas / Negros Oriental | Tanjay settlement and mortuary archaeology. | Best conflict-specific case, but its mass burial is disputed and not representative of all Visayas. |
| Northeastern Mindanao / Butuan | Eighth- to tenth-century boats and exchange-related archaeology. | Strong for mobility and boatbuilding; no direct naval-combat evidence. |
| Other Mindanao and Sulu zone | Deep-past research is thinner in this source set than the later documentary record. | Do not fill the gap with later sultanate warfare. |

## Explicit unknowns

The evidence presently does not answer:

- how often communities fought in any century;
- how large a typical raid, war party, coalition, or battle was;
- whether one battle type predominated across the archipelago;
- how commanders signaled during contact;
- how forces scouted or guarded camps;
- how a routed force was pursued;
- whether there were formal rules for surrender or ransom before 1521;
- whether any society maintained a standing army;
- how naval combat was arranged; or
- whether formal one-versus-one duels were an institution.

These are research results, not omissions to be repaired through imagination.

## Source criticism

- **Archaeological visibility:** Organic shields, wooden defenses, cordage,
  clothing, and boats usually decay in tropical conditions. Surviving stone,
  ceramic, bone, and metal distort the picture.
- **Burial ambiguity:** Grave treatment represents choices by survivors.
  Weapons or detached crania may express status, ritual, violence, or several
  meanings at once.
- **Mixed-method models:** Junker's chiefdom reconstruction is unusually
  valuable because it integrates archaeology and documents, but that strength
  also requires readers to distinguish excavated observations from
  contact-era analogy.
- **Linguistic time depth:** A proto-form demonstrates a historical lexical
  relationship, not a dateable event or preserved institution.
- **Regional sampling:** Tanjay, Batanes, and Butuan are cases, not aliases for
  the archipelago.
- **Modern continuity:** Modern Filipino martial arts and twentieth-century
  ethnography are not evidence of unchanged pre-1521 battle technique without
  a demonstrated transmission chain.

## Planning takeaways

- Treat **raiding, defense, alliance, and maritime mobility** as candidate
  strategic contexts, with regional settings named.
- Treat **portable wealth, captives, political reputation, and access to
  exchange** as possible late pre-contact objectives, not universal motives.
- Model a large force, if needed, as a **coalition problem** before treating it
  as a formation problem.
- Make **terrain and approach routes** more historically defensible than
  invented formation names.
- Preserve uncertainty: the deep-past track can support different combat
  situations, but not one precise universal doctrine.
