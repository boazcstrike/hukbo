# Deep Past, Depth 2: Forces and Command

## Purpose

This document narrows the deep-past track from warfare systems to the people
who could take part: leaders, followers, allied contingents, boat crews,
equipment holders, and settlement defenders. It distinguishes archaeological
evidence for hierarchy and cooperation from unsupported claims about armies,
ranks, drill, and command signals.

## Scope and evidence ceiling

The strongest organizational evidence concerns late first- and
second-millennium coastal or riverine chiefdoms, especially Junker's Tanjay
case. Batanes contributes evidence for fortified settlement; Butuan contributes
boatbuilding evidence. None preserves a muster roll, rank list, order of
battle, training manual, or command transcript.

Evidence labels:

- **Attested** — directly present in relevant material or linguistic evidence.
- **Strong reconstruction** — several evidence types support the conclusion.
- **Plausible inference** — consistent with those constraints but unobserved.
- **Unknown or unsupported** — no adequate deep-past evidence.

## What kind of political organization could raise a force?

### Ranked communities and chiefly centers

**Attested materially:** Protohistoric burials show uneven access to imported
ceramics, metal tools, ornaments, glass beads, and other goods. The
[National Museum](https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/2021/11/23/burial-goods/)
describes this period's mortuary record as more stratified than the broad
Metal Age contrast between richer and poorer graves. Tanjay archaeology also
shows differences among settlements and neighborhoods in prestige goods,
production, and burial treatment
([Junker 1999](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt6wr1cq)).

**Strong reconstruction, late pre-contact chiefdom settings:** Leaders did not
command simply through an impersonal state office. Junker's regional synthesis
models authority as personalized and continually reproduced through kinship,
exchange, feasting, ritual standing, redistribution, and success in trade and
war. The political unit was therefore potentially segmentary: followers and
lesser leaders could cooperate without losing their own relationships and
interests.

This organizational model supports:

- a central war sponsor or leading chief;
- close followers whose loyalty was tied directly to that leader;
- subordinate or allied leaders with their own followers;
- temporary coalition forces; and
- unequal access to prestigious equipment or protection.

It does **not** establish the indigenous title of every role or a fixed chain
of command.

### Coalition-building

**Strong reconstruction:** Archaeological evidence for feasting and unequal
distribution of imported goods supports political events in which leaders
converted food, valuables, and ritual hospitality into obligations and
alliances. Junker and Niziolek specifically connect such networks to marriage,
trade alliances, and war coalition-building
([2010, pp. 17-53](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/289944019_Food_Preparation_and_Feasting_in_the_Household_and_Political_Economy_of_Pre-hispanic_Philippine_Chiefdoms)).

**Plausible inference:** A late pre-contact force could be assembled by calling
in existing personal obligations and offering a share in anticipated returns.
Different contingents might remain most responsive to the leader who recruited
them.

**Unknown or unsupported:** No deep-past source gives a standard recruiting
age, service obligation, levy quota, payment rate, booty share, or penalty for
refusal.

## A minimum force model

The following hierarchy is the smallest cautious abstraction that fits the
evidence. It is **not an attested organization chart**.

| Layer | Evidence-based interpretation | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Campaign sponsor / leading chief | A politically successful leader could mobilize allies and followers. | **Strong reconstruction** in late chiefdom contexts |
| Contingent leader | An allied or subordinate leader probably retained authority over personally connected followers. | **Plausible inference** |
| Close follower | A person tied directly by kinship, obligation, reward, or reputation. | **Strong reconstruction** as a social relation; military duties unspecified |
| Additional participant | Settlement inhabitant, boat crew member, dependent, or ally contributing to movement, supply, defense, or fighting. | **Plausible inference** |

Future agents should not convert these layers automatically into "general,"
"captain," "sergeant," and "soldier." Those terms imply formal ranks,
delegated offices, and standardized spans of control that the evidence does
not establish.

## Force composition

### Fighters

**Attested, but not numerically:** Metal Age and Protohistoric archaeological
collections include spearheads and other metal tools. The National Museum
notes copper and bronze adzes and spearheads from sites including Bacong,
Mulanay, and Maitum
([National Museum tools collection](https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/our-collections/archaeology/tools/)).
The Proto-Philippine reconstruction `*bákal` connects warfare with spearing or
throwing a spear, though the cognate set has semantic uncertainty
([ACD `*bákal`](https://acd.clld.org/cognatesets/33998)).

These sources support people familiar with weapons, especially spears. They do
not establish a separate full-time warrior profession for every region.

### Boat crews

**Attested capability:** Eighth- to tenth-century Butuan boats required
specialized construction knowledge and coordinated operation. The preserved
hulls establish water transport and technical cooperation
([National Museum 2022, including its Lacsina
bibliography](https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/2022/10/24/preserving-our-heritage-towards-the-future-of-our-museums/)).

**Plausible inference:** People who propelled, steered, navigated, landed, and
maintained a boat were part of the operational force even if not every crew
member fought.

**Unknown or unsupported:** A permanent distinction between sailor and
warrior, the crew complement, watch system, embarkation order, and combat
stationing.

### Defenders and noncombat support

**Plausible inference:** People who were not primary fighters still had to
move food and water, ready boats, warn others, shelter dependents, maintain
fortified places, carry equipment, and care for injured people. These are
requirements of movement and survival, not attested job titles.

**Unknown or unsupported:** Specific sex, age, status, or occupational rules
for these tasks. Mortuary and later documentary evidence should not be turned
into a universal gender roster.

### Specialists

The deep-past record does not securely identify:

- dedicated scouts;
- signalers;
- medics;
- armorers attached to a field force;
- siege engineers;
- professional missile troops;
- bodyguard units;
- military priests or ritual officers; or
- permanent naval crews.

Any of these may have existed in a particular society. Their absence from this
model is an evidence limit, not proof of nonexistence.

## Equipment and unequal access

### Weapons

**Attested:** Archaeological metal collections include spearheads and cutting
or working tools. Burials sometimes include metal implements. Late
pre-contact Tanjay graves include objects interpreted in relation to warrior
status
([Junker 1999](https://www.jstor.org/stable/29792432)).

**Plausible inference:** Weapon quality, metal content, decoration, and access
could vary with wealth and political position. A leader's redistributive
network could help followers acquire scarce metal or prestige equipment.

**Unknown or unsupported:**

- standardized issue;
- identical weapon dimensions;
- uniform loadouts;
- archipelago-wide names for blade classes;
- required weapon by social rank; and
- a fixed ratio of spear, blade, bow, or shield users.

The sixteenth-century inventory in
[`HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md`](../HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md) belongs to the
early-contact evidence track. It may suggest research questions, but it cannot
be copied backward as a deep-past roster.

### Protection

**Unknown or unsupported at useful tactical resolution:** Organic armor and
shields preserve poorly. The absence of an excavated shield does not prove
that no shields existed; later shields do not prove that an identical design
was used centuries earlier. No deep-past source in this set supports uniform
armor classes, shield dimensions, or coverage probabilities.

### Status display

**Strong reconstruction, late Tanjay context:** Grave treatment, ornaments,
imported goods, and unusual mortuary associations could mark social standing
and a person's remembered relationship to violence or leadership. Junker's
"warrior burial" interpretation is informative but not self-proving; Aure's
critique shows that mortuary deposits can be multivocal
([Aure 2004](https://www.jstor.org/stable/29792554)).

For a simulation, visible prestige may reasonably influence perceived
leadership or morale as a **design hypothesis**. It is not evidence that all
fighters wore rank insignia.

## Command, control, and cohesion

### Before contact

**Strong reconstruction:** Coalition formation depended on relationships,
reputation, exchange, and shared benefit. Command authority was therefore
social before it was spatial.

**Plausible inference:** A leader could make a plan before movement, identify
an objective, select an approach, and rely on known subleaders. Smaller groups
could act with more mutual awareness than a large coalition.

### During contact

**Unknown or unsupported:** No deep-past source establishes:

- shouted command vocabulary;
- horns, gongs, drums, flags, fires, or other battle signals;
- messenger organization;
- prearranged maneuvers;
- command radius;
- a formal reserve;
- replacement of a fallen leader; or
- how allied leaders resolved contradictory orders.

Sound, gesture, and runners are mechanically plausible, but selecting one as
historical fact would exceed the evidence.

### Cohesion

**Plausible inference grounded in the political model:** Cohesion likely
depended on several overlapping bonds:

- personal attachment to a leader;
- kinship and residence;
- shared risk in the same boat or approach group;
- expectation of reward or redistribution;
- mutual defense; and
- reputation before peers.

This supports a simulation variable for cohesion more safely than it supports
a formal drill system. Cohesion should be local and relational, not an
automatic national characteristic.

## Logistics

### Movement

**Attested capability:** The Butuan boats demonstrate that large plank-built
watercraft existed by the late first millennium in northeastern Mindanao.
The Batanes record demonstrates sustained inter-island contact over a much
longer sequence
([Bellwood and Dizon 2013](https://doi.org/10.22459/TA40.12.2013)).

**Strong reconstruction:** Coastal and river movement could concentrate a
group faster than overland travel through broken terrain, while tides,
weather, landing places, and boat availability constrained timing.

### Food, water, and duration

**Plausible inference:** A larger or longer expedition required more food,
fresh water, carrying capacity, and coordination. Portable-raiding objectives
reduced the need to feed an occupying force for long periods.

**Unknown or unsupported:** Ration types, march rates, camp layouts, supply
train organization, resupply doctrine, and maximum operational duration.

### Fortified places

**Attested, Batanes:** An *ijang* could gather inhabitants on defensible high
ground
([Lacsina 2009](https://pages.upd.edu.ph/sites/default/files/asp/files/lacsina_thesis.pdf)).

**Plausible inference:** Concentrating people improves defense but also
increases immediate demand for water, food, and space. A refuge that delays an
attacker is not necessarily a permanently garrisoned fortress.

## What happens when the force becomes large?

As participant count grows, three problems grow with it:

1. **Assembly:** people and boats must reach the same place and time.
2. **Communication:** fewer participants can directly perceive the leading
   chief.
3. **Commitment:** allied contingents may assess danger and reward
   differently.

These are **strong general constraints** and **plausible historical
inferences**, not recovered doctrine.

A cautious large-force representation would use:

- several locally cohesive contingents;
- one recognized campaign sponsor;
- limited direct control outside the sponsor's immediate group;
- delays or misunderstandings between contingents;
- stronger loyalty to local leaders than to an abstract army; and
- possible withdrawal when a contingent's leader, boat, or expected reward is
  lost.

No exact group size, command radius, or morale value is historically attested.

## Explicit unknowns

The deep-past evidence does not establish:

- standing versus seasonal service;
- training frequency;
- force-size categories;
- elite bodyguard numbers;
- rank names or badges;
- recruitment by age, class, or sex;
- command succession;
- standardized weapons;
- disciplinary punishment;
- tactical signals;
- camp security procedures;
- casualty evacuation; or
- naval crew and fighting complements.

## Planning takeaways

| Candidate system | Historical basis | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leader-centered cohesion | Personalized chiefly networks | **Strong reconstruction**, late chiefdom contexts |
| Allied contingents | Segmentary coalition model | **Plausible inference** |
| Prestige affects following | Unequal goods and redistributive politics | **Plausible inference**, not a numeric law |
| Boat-dependent mobilization | Butuan boats and maritime exchange | **Strong reconstruction** for mobility, not combat geometry |
| Equipment inequality | Stratified burials and metal access | **Plausible inference** |
| Formal ranks and drill | None | **Unknown or unsupported** |
| Command signals | None | **Unknown or unsupported** |
