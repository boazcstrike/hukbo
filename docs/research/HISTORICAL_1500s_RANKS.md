# Historical Philippine Social Standing and Warrior Obligation, 1500s

Research date: 2026-07-29

Purpose: provide an evidence record for how sixteenth-century Philippine
societies ranked the people who appeared at a fight, what each rank owed,
and what each rank was given. This document does not define gameplay stats,
combat balance, or final historical reconstructions. It is the standing
counterpart to
[`HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md`](HISTORICAL_1500s_WEAPONS.md) and follows the
same confidence labels, the same pair-form naming rule, and the same
hundred-year attestation rule.

## The short answer to "how strong is each rank?"

No sixteenth-century source ranks fighting ability by social class. Not one
of the accounts consulted here gives a rank a combat bonus, a training
regime, a weapon proficiency, a survival rate, or any other measure that
could be read as a strength value. The word "rank" in the modern military
sense — a graded office within a standing army, with a defined span of
command — is explicitly unsupported for this period; the deep-past research
track already records the same verdict from the archaeological side (see
[`battles/02-deep-past-forces-and-command.md`](battles/02-deep-past-forces-and-command.md),
"Formal ranks and drill: **Unknown or unsupported**").

What the sources do document, and document well, is a *social and legal*
hierarchy with four consequences that a battle simulation can honestly use:

1. **Obligation.** Who was required to turn up, armed, when a chief called.
2. **Equipment access.** Who could reach gold, dyed cloth, and metal, and
   who could not.
3. **Following.** How many people a given leader could bring, and why that
   number varied.
4. **Reward.** Who took the spoils and who took a share of them.

Every one of those four is attested. None of them is a personal strength
statistic. A design that expresses standing through obligation, equipment,
following, and reward is standing on evidence. A design that hands a noble a
damage multiplier and calls it history is not, and this document will not be
cited to support one.

## Research boundary

"The Philippines in the 1500s" was not one society with one vocabulary. The
three strongest accounts used here describe three overlapping but distinct
situations, and they disagree with one another in ways that matter:

- **Miguel de Loarca, *Relación de las Yslas Filipinas*, Arévalo, 1582** —
  the Visayas, written by a settler-encomendero who had been in the islands
  since the first years.
- **Juan de Plasencia, *Customs of the Tagalogs*, 1589** — the Tagalog
  region around Manila and Laguna, written by a Franciscan compiling
  customary law for colonial administration.
- **Antonio de Morga, *Sucesos de las Islas Filipinas*, Mexico, 1609** —
  a retrospective overview by a senior colonial judge, describing conditions
  he places before and during the conquest.

All three are colonial documents produced to make an occupied population
legible for tribute, labour assignment, and litigation. They are useful
evidence about obligation and status because obligation and status were
exactly what the colonial administration needed to write down. They are not
neutral ethnography, they flatten regional variation, and each author maps
local categories onto Spanish ones — Plasencia calls chiefs "knights",
Morga calls freemen "plebeians". Read the categories as the Spanish
administrative translation of something local, never as the local thing
itself.

### Confidence labels

The same three labels the weapons document uses:

- **Documented:** directly described in a sixteenth- or
  early-seventeenth-century source.
- **Documented, form uncertain:** the category is attested, but its exact
  local form, extent, or the attachment of a particular name to it is not.
- **Provisional reconstruction:** a plausible identification supported by
  later traditions, but not firmly established for the period.

A fourth outcome is used where it is honest: **not verified in this pass**,
for a term that is widely repeated in modern secondary writing but that does
not appear in any text actually opened for this document.

## What the sources say

### Tagalog: Plasencia, 1589

Plasencia describes a barangay as a kin group — "a family of parents and
children, relations and slaves" — and is explicit that these groups were not
subordinate to one another: "They were not, however, subject to one another,
except in friendship and relationship. The chiefs, in their various wars,
helped one another with their respective barangays." That single sentence is
the closest thing in the record to an order of battle: a force is a
coalition of kin groups, each following its own chief.

Below the chiefs he names three castes.

**Maharlika — the free-born.** Documented, verbatim:

> "The nobles were the free-born whom they call maharlica. They did not pay
> tax or tribute to the dato, but must accompany him in war, at their own
> expense. The chief offered them beforehand a feast, and afterward they
> divided the spoils."

Four facts in one passage: exemption from tribute, a *military* obligation
rather than a fiscal one, self-equipment, and a share of the spoils. The
same passage adds that they rowed for the chief when he went on the water,
helped build his house, and cleared his land. Plasencia also records that a
maharlika could not move between villages after marriage without paying a
fine in gold, and that failure to pay could start a war between the two
barangays — a bond of following, enforced.

**Aliping namamahay — the householder dependents.** Documented:

> "The commoners are called aliping namamahay. They are married, and serve
> their master, whether he be a dato or not, with half of their cultivated
> lands… They accompanied him whenever he went beyond the island, and rowed
> for him. They live in their own houses, and are lords of their property
> and gold."

They could not be sold and could not be moved out of their own village.
Their service is described as agricultural and maritime; the text gives them
no armed obligation of the kind the maharlika carry.

**Aliping sa guiguilir — the household dependents.** Documented:

> "The slaves are called aliping sa guiguilir. They serve their master in
> his house and on his cultivated lands, and may be sold."

Plasencia's own summary is that these are three castes — "nobles, commoners,
and slaves" — sitting under the chiefs, "who corresponded to our knights".
The English words are his translation, and neither "noble" nor "slave"
carries its European meaning intact; both classes of *alipin* held property,
and the namamahay held land and gold.

**The maharlika misconception.** In modern Philippine usage *maharlika* is
frequently used to mean "nobility" or "royalty". Plasencia's own text does
not support that: his maharlika are the free-born who owe war service to the
dato and are distinct from the chiefly class above them. The popularised
"nobility" reading is a twentieth-century development and must not be used
in this project. This is a naming trap of exactly the kind the weapons
document warns about, and it is the single most likely error for anyone
adding rank content from general knowledge.

### Visayas: Loarca, 1582

Loarca gives the same three-part shape with different words and, critically,
a different meaning for the middle term.

**Timagua — the bound freeman.** Documented, and the most directly military
passage in any of the three sources:

> "The freemen of these islands, who are called timaguas, are neither chiefs
> nor slaves… If a timagua desires to live in a certain village, he joins
> himself to one of the chiefs — for each village usually has many chiefs,
> each of whom has his own district, with slaves and timaguas, well known to
> him — to whom he offers himself as his timagua… He must, with his weapons,
> accompany the chief when he goes on a journey. When the latter enters a
> boat the timagua must go to ply the oar, and to carry his weapons for the
> defense of the vessel."

The relationship is reciprocal and voluntary: "For this service the chief is
under obligation to defend the timagua, in his own person and those of his
relatives, against anyone who seeks to injure him without cause… Thus the
timaguas live in security, and are free to pass from the service of one
chief to that of another, whenever they so desire, and without any obstacle
being placed in their way."

On reward, Loarca is precise and unflattering: "The booty that they take,
whatever it may be, belongs to the chiefs, except a small portion which is
given to the timaguas who go with them as oarsmen." Where several chiefs
raided together, the one who had made the sacrifice took half the booty and
the rest divided the other half.

**Three grades of dependency.** Documented, with values:

| Loarca's term | Obligation | Recorded value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ayuey | Lives in the master's dwelling; works three days for the master and one for himself; the only grade fed and clothed by the master | two gold *taes*, "the equivalent of twelve pesos" |
| Tumaranpoque | Lives in his own house; works one day in four, or gives ten *chicubites* of rice a year | the same, twelve pesos |
| Tumataban | "Whom these people hold in most respect"; works only at a banquet or revel; five days a month, or five *chicubites* of rice a year | one *tae*, six pesos |

These are the only numbers in the entire body of evidence that grade one
class against another, and they are **prices and labour-days, not fighting
values**. They are recorded here because they are the closest the sources
come to quantifying anything about standing, and because a reader looking
for "how strong is each rank" deserves to see exactly how far the evidence
actually goes.

Loarca also notes that the timaguas had a standing in village life beyond
labour: one "who is held in honor" carried the public announcement of a
chief's death, and chiefs sent respected timaguas as marriage go-betweens.

### Overview: Morga, 1609

Morga gives the three-part division as a general rule — "There are three
conditions of persons among the natives of these islands, and into which
their government is divided: the chiefs, of whom we have already treated;
the timaguas, who are equivalent to plebeians; and slaves, those of both
chiefs and timaguas."

Note the collision. Morga's *timagua* is a plebeian commoner; Loarca's
*timagua* is a freeman who has sworn armed service to a named chief. The
word is the same and the social position is not. Any player-facing use of
these terms must be scoped to a region, exactly as the appearance research
already scopes costume presets, or the game will assert a uniformity the
sources deny.

Two passages in Morga carry more design weight than anything else in this
document.

**Following is earned, and it scales with war record.** Documented:

> "When any of these chiefs was more courageous than others in war and upon
> other occasions, such a one enjoyed more followers and men; and the others
> were under his leadership, even if they were chiefs."

This is the mechanism the record actually supports: distinction in war
converts into *a larger following and authority over other leaders*, not
into a personal combat bonus. A leading chief remains one body with one
blade; what changes is how many people stand with him.

**Service was owed to the chief personally.** Documented: chiefs were "held
in great veneration and respect; and they were served in their wars and
voyages, and in their tilling, sowing, fishing, and the building of their
houses. To these duties the natives attended very promptly, whenever
summoned by their chief." Descendants and relatives of chiefs kept the
respect and the exemption from service even without inheriting the
lordship.

**Tattoo and prominence.** Documented, with a caution. Morga explains the
name *Pintados* by saying that among the Visayans "the more prominent of the
men, from their youth, tattoo their whole bodies". He ties tattooing to
prominence and to youth. He does **not** say in this passage that each
tattoo records a specific war deed; that per-deed reading rests on later
seventeenth-century writers and on modern synthesis, and the appearance
research already carries the visual side of this question with its own
tiers. Treat "tattoo indicates prominence" as Documented and "tattoo counts
kills" as **not verified in this pass**.

## What the sources do not say

Stated plainly, because a rank feature is unusually easy to over-claim:

- **No source gives a fighting-strength difference between classes.** No
  hit-rate, no endurance, no weapon skill, no casualty asymmetry.
- **No source describes training, drill, or a military academy** for any
  class. Familiarity with weapons is inferred from the weapons themselves.
- **No source gives a muster roll, a force size by class, or a ratio** of
  chiefs to freemen to dependents in a fighting force. Loarca's population
  figures are tribute-collection estimates for whole islands and villages,
  not orders of battle.
- **No source describes a badge, insignia, or uniform of military rank.**
  Status display is wealth display — gold, dyed cloth, ornament — and it is
  the appearance research, not this document, that governs how it is drawn.
- **No source describes promotion, demotion, or a career.** Standing is
  inherited, married into, or lost through debt and crime; the routes into
  and out of dependency that the sources actually detail are all economic
  and legal, never martial.
- **The three vocabularies do not reconcile.** Tagalog *maharlika* is not
  Visayan *timagua*, and Visayan *timagua* is not Morga's *timagua*. There
  is no archipelago-wide rank ladder to model.

## Naming policy for this project

The rules in `CLAUDE.md` section 7 apply to standing terms exactly as they
apply to weapon names.

- A standing term never appears bare in player-facing UI. It appears in
  **pair form** — Filipino name, em dash, plain English descriptor — with the
  evidence tier available in the agent inspector.
- A term whose earliest attestation postdates the depicted period by more
  than a century is not used at all.
- A term is scoped to the region whose source attests it. No term is
  presented as "the Philippine" name for anything.

### Terms cleared for use, with tiers

| Pair-form label | Region | Tier | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Datu — Chief | Tagalog and Visayan | Documented | Plasencia's *dato*, Loarca's chiefs, Morga's *datos*. The best-attested term in the set. |
| Maharlika — Sworn Freeman | Tagalog | Documented | Plasencia 1589, verbatim, including the war obligation. The descriptor deliberately avoids "noble", which carries Plasencia's Spanish gloss rather than his content, and avoids the modern "royalty" misreading entirely. |
| Timawa — Bound Freeman | Visayan | Documented | Loarca 1582, spelled *timagua*. Descriptor reflects the sworn, transferable bond to a named chief. Must not be used for the Tagalog region, where Morga's sense is different. |
| Namamahay — Householder | Tagalog | Documented | Plasencia's *aliping namamahay*. Descriptor reflects the documented fact that they held their own houses, land, and gold. |
| Ayuey — Household Dependent | Visayan | Documented, form uncertain | Loarca 1582. The category and its obligations are recorded; the spelling is Loarca's own transcription and the modern orthography is unsettled. |

### Terms deliberately excluded

- **Bagani / magani** (Mindanao warrior grade). The obvious candidate for a
  "proven warrior" tier, and excluded for the same reason *panabas* is
  excluded from the armory: its documentation is nineteenth- and
  twentieth-century ethnography of interior Mindanao societies, roughly
  three centuries after the depicted period, and none of the
  sixteenth-century sources consulted here uses it. The hundred-year rule
  excludes it outright rather than badging it provisional.
- **Maharlika meaning "nobility" or "royalty".** A twentieth-century usage
  that contradicts the 1589 text. The word is cleared for use; that meaning
  is not.
- **Tumao, oripun, horohan.** Widely repeated in modern secondary writing on
  Visayan society. None of them appears in the Loarca, Plasencia, or Morga
  texts opened for this document. They are recorded here as **not verified
  in this pass** and are excluded until someone opens a source that carries
  them. Loarca's own words for the dependent grades — *ayuey*,
  *tumaranpoque*, *tumataban* — are attested and are what this project uses.
- **Sultan, rajah, lakan.** Real titles with real sixteenth-century
  attestation, but they name particular paramount rulers and polities rather
  than a rank a rostered warrior could hold. Out of scope for a battle
  roster; revisit only if a campaign layer ever needs polity heads.
- **Any rank name for a command grade** — captain, sergeant, lieutenant, or a
  Filipino equivalent invented to fill the slot. The sources support a
  leader and his personal following, and nothing between.

## Gaps and unknowns

- **Whether dependents fought at all is uncertain.** Plasencia gives the
  namamahay maritime and agricultural service and reserves the explicit war
  obligation for the maharlika. Loarca has the timagua carry weapons for the
  defence of the vessel. Whether a household dependent was ever put in a
  battle line is an inference either way, and a roster that fields them must
  say in the inspector that this is a reconstruction.
- **Women in combat.** This rank-focused source set does not resolve combat
  composition. A later focused review finds organized war service to be
  normatively male, while direct defensive participation by women remains
  documented but uncertain in form. See
  [Gender and Warrior Composition in the Late-Sixteenth-Century
  Philippines](HISTORICAL_1500s_WARRIOR_GENDER.md). Do not infer combat duty
  from female membership in a social grade.
- **Regional coverage is uneven.** The Visayas and the Tagalog region
  dominate. Mindanao, Sulu, and the Cordillera are thin or absent in this
  source set and are deliberately unmodelled.
- **Terminology and spelling are the transcribers'.** *Aliping sa guiguilir*,
  *tumaranpoque*, *timagua* are Spanish renderings of spoken words by
  authors with no standard orthography.
- **The Blair and Robertson translation is itself an interpretation.** These
  are early-twentieth-century English translations of sixteenth-century
  Spanish, with editorial footnotes of their period. Where a single word is
  load-bearing, the Spanish should be checked before it is built on.
- **Not opened in this pass:** William Henry Scott, *Barangay:
  Sixteenth-Century Philippine Culture and Society* (1994), the standard
  modern synthesis; Francisco Ignacio Alcina's 1668 Visayan history; and the
  Boxer Codex text. Each is likely to sharpen or correct several rows above,
  and any claim sourced to them must be added with its own tier and its own
  citation rather than folded silently into the tables here.
